  - question: >-
      Nigeria’s transition from the Nigerian Shippers’ Council to NPERA
      represents one of the most significant changes in the economic regulation
      of the ports. From the perspective of a customs broker, what should NPERA
      change first about the experience of moving cargo through Nigerian ports,
      particularly around charges, delays, commercial disputes and
      accountability?
    answer: >-
      <p class="MsoNormal">What we need from <b><a href="#" target="_blank"
      rel="noopener noreferrer">NPERA</a></b>, first and foremost, is clarity.

      Clarity about transparency, promotion of efficiency in the value chain and

      accountability in the Nigerian port system.<o:p></o:p></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">Transparency means having clarity about who does what
      and

      what the implications of non-performance are. Most importantly, there
      needs to

      be a proper mechanism for the resolution of disputes. This has been at the

      lowest ebb, and no one has been responsive or accountable as a go-to

      institution when disputes arise.<o:p></o:p></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">A major part of the disputes that arise from the port

      concerns charges and getting paid for non-performance. So, when I talk
      about

      accountability and clarity, I am talking about charges and
      performance.<o:p></o:p></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">The most important protection any shipper expects
      from <a href="#" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"><b>NPERA

      </b></a>is clarity around charges and performance.<o:p></o:p></p>
  - question: >-
      Customs is increasingly emphasising compliance, risk management and
      Post-Clearance Audit rather than relying solely on physical intervention
      at the port. What does this shift mean for customs brokers and importers
      in practical terms, and are businesses sufficiently prepared for a system
      where documentation and declarations may face greater scrutiny after cargo
      has already been released?
    answer: >-
      <p class="MsoNormal">Customs administration globally and freight
      forwarding are

      international and rule based. I commend the present leadership of the
      Nigeria

      Customs Service for trying to match international best
      practices.<o:p></o:p></p><p class="MsoNormal">There is no problem with
      Nigerian businesses and the average

      shipper being accountable from a documentation standpoint. The onus is on

      practitioners to advocate, while the average shipper also needs to
      understand

      that international trade is accountable and rule-based.<o:p></o:p></p><p
      class="MsoNormal">Post Clearance Audit gives Customs an opportunity to
      focus

      its personnel and machinery on high risk cargo while allowing compliant

      shippers to exit within the required time and remain accountable after

      clearance.<o:p></o:p></p><p class="MsoNormal">What I expect to see is more
      advocacy and sensitisation by

      the Nigeria Customs Service. Customs needs to take this message to every
      nook

      and cranny of the country. They need to go into the marketplace, not just
      hold

      meetings in five star hotels.<o:p></o:p></p><p class="MsoNormal">








      <span
      style="font-size:11.0pt;line-height:115%;font-family:&quot;Calibri&quot;,sans-serif;

      mso-fareast-font-family:SimSun;mso-bidi-font-family:&quot;Times New
      Roman&quot;;

      mso-ansi-language:EN-US;mso-fareast-language:ZH-CN;mso-bidi-language:AR-SA">Nigeria

      is import dependent, and a significant proportion of the shipping
      community

      operates from the markets. If Customs properly sensitises these
      market-based

      shippers on compliance requirements, it can focus its resources on
      high-risk

      cargo while compliant shippers experience easier and faster
      clearance.</span></p>
  - question: >-
      The National Single Window and broader digitalisation of trade processes
      are intended to reduce duplication, physical interaction and clearance
      delays. From your experience on the ground, where is digitalisation
      genuinely improving cargo clearance today, and where are manual processes,
      multiple agencies or inconsistent requirements still undermining the
      objective?
    answer: >-
      <p class="MsoNormal">The Single Window is supposed to create a seamless
      process

      where information is created and shared among the relevant government
      agencies.

      Progress is being made gradually, but manual processes and duplication are

      still very much present.<o:p></o:p></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">One of the major problems is the rivalry and
      conflicting

      mandates among government regulatory agencies.<o:p></o:p></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">For instance, you can have agencies such as the
      National

      Agency for Food and Drug Administration and Control (NAFDAC) and the
      Standards

      Organisation of Nigeria (SON) both regulating products such as paint,
      diapers

      and table salt. There needs to be a clear separation of boundaries and a

      unitary command around the regulation of each product.<o:p></o:p></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">There are also instances where the National
      Environmental

      Standards and Regulations Enforcement Agency (NESREA) and other agencies
      have

      regulatory interests around certain controlled products. These overlapping

      mandates need to be clearly defined.<o:p></o:p></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">When I was the National Secretary of the Association
      of

      Nigerian Licensed Customs Agents (ANLCA), we identified more than 40
      products

      where there were conflicting mandates. There have been efforts to resolve
      these

      issues by getting agencies with overlapping regulatory responsibilities to

      share responsibilities, but the challenges persist.<o:p></o:p></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">The Ministry of Trade needs to do more because these

      overlapping mandates continue to hamper trade. The Presidential Enabling

      Business Environment Council has done what it can, but the problem has not
      been

      completely resolved.<o:p></o:p></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">The Customs Service, as the lead agency in the
      clearance

      process, can itself be hampered by these conflicting
      mandates.<o:p></o:p></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">The Single Window needs sufficient authority to bring

      critical stakeholders together and identify the borderline regulatory and

      oversight arguments around individual products. That is how you create a
      truly

      seamless system.<o:p></o:p></p>
  - question: >-
      One of the biggest concerns for importers is not simply the statutory cost
      of bringing goods into Nigeria, but the uncertainty created by delays,
      additional charges, examinations, valuation issues, container related
      costs and changing regulatory requirements. What are the biggest avoidable
      costs in the current system, and what specific reforms would make the
      final cost of clearing cargo more predictable?
    answer: >-
      <p class="MsoNormal">I am optimistic that Nigeria can get there if the
      government

      identifies the logs in our wheel of progress and unbundles those
      bottlenecks.<o:p></o:p></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">My biggest concern is the non-performance of critical

      elements of the value chain and the absence of transparent
      protocols.<o:p></o:p></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">The World Customs Organization and the World Trade

      Organization preach transparency and predictability. How can we get

      transparency when charges are hidden? <br><br>How can there be rule-based
      clarity on

      who pays for what, when the performance of terminal operators is not clear
      and

      there is no simple, rule-based dispute mechanism when a service has been
      paid

      for but not delivered?<o:p></o:p></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">Another major concern is valuation.<o:p></o:p></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">There are established principles of Customs
      valuation, but I

      do not think Customs is sufficiently accountable to those principles.

      Transaction value appears to have been jettisoned in
      practice.<o:p></o:p></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">You see a Customs officer use a phone to show what is

      described as the window or showroom price in China and then use that as a
      basis

      for valuation. That negates the transactional value from the manufacturer
      or

      factory.<o:p></o:p></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">Prices found online, including Alibaba prices, can
      represent

      retail or showroom prices and may include additional costs. They are not

      necessarily the actual transaction value of the goods being
      imported.<o:p></o:p></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">With the networking capability available to the
      Customs

      Service under its present leadership, there should be better ways of

      determining the actual value of shipments. We need a more finely tuned
      strategy

      based on facts that can establish the true transaction
      value.<o:p></o:p></p>


      <blockquote><p class="MsoNormal">Accountability should begin with Customs
      showing the basis

      on which a valuation has been arrived at.<o:p></o:p></p></blockquote>


      <p class="MsoNormal">Ultimately, the foundation has to be efficient, rule
      based

      international trade practice across the entire value chain.<o:p></o:p></p>
  - question: >-
      Looking ahead, what would a genuinely modern Nigerian cargo clearance and
      port system look like from the perspective of the customs broker? With the
      recent lifting of the United States' 12 year Condition of Entry
      restrictions on vessels calling at Nigerian ports, Nigeria has an
      opportunity to strengthen its position in international shipping and
      trade. What must NPERA, Customs, the ports, freight forwarders and other
      agencies do differently to translate this improved international standing
      into greater shipping activity, lower trade costs and a more competitive
      Nigerian logistics hub?
    answer: >-
      <p class="MsoNormal">I am excited about NPERA. Gone are the days when we
      talked

      about the Nigerian Shippers' Council as a toothless bulldog. NPERA has a

      broader mandate, and it has the capacity to sue and be sued. There are
      also

      penalties, including provisions for first offences, subsequent offences,

      corporate liability and individual liability.<o:p></o:p></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">NPERA should focus on pricing first. We need to
      reduce the

      cost of doing business at the Nigerian port and, by extension, the cost of

      doing business in Nigeria.<o:p></o:p></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">There must be clarity. We need to unbundle the
      bottlenecks

      and have transparency around protocols and processes.<o:p></o:p></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">Pricing should be transparent. Nothing should be
      hidden.<o:p></o:p></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">A global practitioner should be able to know the
      turnaround

      time, the cost of berthing a vessel, the number of hours required for

      particular services and the cost per hour or per day. Every ship calling
      at a

      Nigerian port should know exactly what to expect.<o:p></o:p></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">There should also be no non-state actors involved in

      critical processes such as ship boarding and berthing.<o:p></o:p></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal"><a href="#" target="_blank" rel="noopener
      noreferrer"><b>NPERA </b></a>should promote efficiency, transparency,

      professionalism and accountability. With the powers now available to it,
      there

      is no excuse for not being able to apportion responsibility and apply
      sanctions

      where necessary.<o:p></o:p></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">We need NPERA to hit the ground running and make
      Nigeria

      competitive within the global value chain.<o:p></o:p></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">Freight forwarders and customs brokers also have a

      responsibility. We need to do more advocacy and ensure that the experience
      of

      practitioners is properly communicated to government and
      regulators.<o:p></o:p></p>
  - question: >-
      Freight forwarders’ associations play an important advocacy role in
      representing practitioners and engaging regulators on issues affecting
      cargo clearance and port operations. From your experience, how effective
      has this advocacy been in influencing reforms and resolving operational
      challenges, and in what ways has the Nigerian port system benefited from
      the industry’s engagement with government and regulatory agencies?
    answer: >-
      <p class="MsoNormal">Advocacy is the baseline reason freight forwarders
      and

      customs brokers associate. It gives practitioners an opportunity to expose

      their concerns and put their experience forward in the interest of a
      better

      port system and operating environment.<o:p></o:p></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">Unfortunately, advocacy has continued to nosedive. We
      have

      seen the emergence of many associations, and some of them appear to have
      lost

      sight of the purpose for which they were established.<o:p></o:p></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">The push-through has not benefited the Nigerian port
      system

      enough, and the associations have to take some responsibility for
      that.<o:p></o:p></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">Many regulations are rolled out without critical

      stakeholders being involved at the planning and drafting stage. Freight

      forwarders are often brought in at the public hearing stage, when the

      substantive work has already been done. That is too late.<o:p></o:p></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">Associations need to see themselves as partners in
      progress

      and as critical advocacy platforms that can show government where the shoe

      pinches.<o:p></o:p></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">But advocacy cannot simply be about raising concerns.
      It has

      to provide practical, fact-based solutions.<o:p></o:p></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">Nigeria has the potential to become a leading African
      trade

      and logistics hub, but strong institutions require strong
      advocacy.<o:p></o:p></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">When you compare Nigerian freight forwarding
      associations

      with their counterparts in other countries, there is still a gap in the

      capacity and quality of their relationship with government.<o:p></o:p></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">Freight forwarders should not be invited simply to
      increase

      numbers at meetings or create a show for the cameras. They should have a

      substantive role in policy development.<o:p></o:p></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal"><o:p>&nbsp;</o:p></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">The Association of Nigerian Licensed Customs Agents
      (ANLCA),

      which is celebrating 72 years, should be able to provide leadership in
      this

      area. We have several associations saying essentially the same things in

      different voices.<o:p></o:p></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">The Council for the Regulation of Freight Forwarding
      in

      Nigeria (CRFFN) also needs to do more to bring the associations together
      and

      create a stronger common voice for the industry.<o:p></o:p></p>

  <p class="MsoNormal">The emergence of the Nigerian Ports Economic Regulatory

  Agency (NPERA), reforms within the Nigeria Customs Service (NCS), the

  development of the National Single Window and renewed attention to efficiency,

  transparency and accountability are reshaping the operating environment for

  businesses and practitioners.<o:p></o:p></p>


  <p class="MsoNormal">For those working directly within the cargo clearance
  chain,

  however, the real measure of reform is not the creation of institutions or the

  announcement of new processes. It is whether cargo moves predictably, charges

  and procedures are transparent, disputes are resolved effectively and

  businesses can determine the actual cost and requirements of moving goods

  through Nigerian ports.<o:p></o:p></p>


  <p class="MsoNormal"><o:p>&nbsp;</o:p></p>


  <p class="MsoNormal">Questions around Customs compliance, Post-Clearance
  Audit,

  digitalisation, overlapping regulatory mandates, valuation, port charges and

  service performance remain central to that experience.<o:p></o:p></p>


  <p class="MsoNormal"><o:p>&nbsp;</o:p>At the same time, the lifting of the
  United States' 12-year

  Condition of Entry restrictions on vessels calling at Nigerian ports present

  an important opportunity for Nigeria's shipping and trade environment.

  Converting that development into greater shipping activity and stronger

  logistics competitiveness will require reforms that are visible not only to

  domestic operators, but also to the international shipping community.</p>


  <p class="MsoNormal"><o:p>&nbsp;</o:p>In this conversation, Abdullazeez

  the perspective of a customs broker and freight forwarding practitioner,

  examining what needs to change across regulation, Customs administration,

  digital trade facilitation, port operations and industry advocacy.</p><p
