  - question: >-
      Nigeria generates substantial demand for maritime transportation, yet
      indigenous participation in international shipping remains a major
      industry concern. From your perspective, what are the fundamental barriers
      preventing Nigerian companies from acquiring, financing and operating
      vessels at competitive scale, and what would it take to move the country
      from dependence on external shipping capacity towards a stronger
      indigenous fleet?
    answer: >-
      <p class="MsoNormal">We need to move from just being a bucket of

      shipping services to becoming a provider of those services. We need to

      progressively become ship maintainers and shipbuilders.</p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">Capacity is not just about the number of

      vessels or putting ships on the water. It is the ability to win a charter,

      operate a ship safely, repay loans on time and repeat the process. If you
      own a

      vessel but cannot run it commercially, there is no value in that
      ownership.</p>


      <p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">&nbsp;</span>Competence matters
      more than tonnage. We

      need competent people in chartering, ship management, voyage estimation
      and

      protection and indemnity insurance. These are areas where the industry
      needs

      more capacity. We have to build our people, our ships and our management

      capabilities together.</p>


      <p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">&nbsp;</span>We should start where
      Nigeria has

      opportunities, such as coastal tankers, offshore support vessels, barges,
      short

      sea shipping and bulk shipping close to the cargo. We can use the Cabotage

      regime and progressively build the capacity to operate in international
      waters.</p>


      <p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">&nbsp;</span>We need to build a
      track record and

      evaluate our initiatives. A contract performed successfully and on time
      becomes

      the basis for securing another contract and financing another vessel.</p>


      <p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">&nbsp;</span>When freight and
      insurance payments go

      abroad, we lose foreign exchange. Nigerian owned tonnage carrying Nigerian

      cargo is a form of import substitution for services. We must build the
      capacity

      to retain more of that value within the country.</p>
  - question: >-
      Building a national shipping fleet requires substantial capital, long term
      investment decisions and a commercially supportive environment. What
      specific responsibilities should government assume in areas such as policy
      consistency, access to maritime finance, fiscal incentives and regulation,
      and what must private investors and shipping companies do differently to
      build commercially viable businesses rather than depend on government
      intervention?
    answer: >-
      <p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">Government, as regulator and
      policymaker,

      should help reduce risk. The private operator must carry the risks it can

      assess and manage. We must not bite more than we can
      chew.<o:p></o:p></span></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">&nbsp;</span></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">We need policy consistency.
      Policies should

      not change with every administration. Where there is funding through the

      Cabotage Vessel Financing Fund (CVFF) or other maritime financing
      mechanisms,

      there should be a transparent process, clear conditions and proper
      monitoring

      of repayment.<o:p></o:p></span></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">&nbsp;</span></p><p
      class="MsoNormal">Government should also reduce port and

      clearance costs, turnaround times, cargo dwell time, demurrage and illegal

      charges. These costs affect the competitiveness of Nigerian shipping
      companies.</p>


      <p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">We need access to foreign exchange
      and long

      tenure funding. Government can also use cargo under its control as an
      anchor

      for Nigerian tonnage. If government has cargo to move, qualified Nigerian

      operators should have an opportunity to compete for it.</span>&nbsp;</p>


      <p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">The private sector also has

      responsibilities.<o:p></o:p></span></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">&nbsp;Investors must be willing to
      commit their own

      money and develop sound business plans. It should not be about rushing to
      apply

      for the CVFF. Operators must understand commercial scale, chartering, cost

      control, safety requirements and the need to secure bankable contracts and

      reliable cargo relationships.</span>&nbsp;</p>


      <p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">Shipping is an international
      business. We

      must be competitive.<o:p></o:p></span></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">&nbsp;</span>Government should not
      own and operate

      ships. We should learn from the experience of the Nigerian National
      Shipping

      Line, which operated from 1959 to 1995. Government should be an enabler,
      not

      the owner of the business.</p>


      <p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">&nbsp;</span>Any support should
      have a defined period

      and measurable conditions. Waivers, guarantees and cargo preferences
      should be

      tied to performance, with clear timelines for ending the support.</p>


      <p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">&nbsp;</span>We also need an
      accountable structure that

      brings the relevant institutions together, including <a
      href="https://www.primeaxisinnovations.com.ng/profiles/nigerian-maritime-administration-and-safety-agency-nimasa"
      target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">NIMASA</a>, the <a
      href="https://www.primeaxisinnovations.com.ng/profiles/nigerian-ports-authority-npa"
      target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Nigerian Ports

      Authority</a>, the Federal Ministry of Marine and Blue Economy, Customs
      and the

      Nigerian Content Development and Monitoring Board, among others. We need
      to

      monitor the process from beginning to end and publish performance
      data.</p>


      <p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">&nbsp;</span>There should be no
      blame game. Government

      should de risk the environment, while the private sector must perform and

      contribute to the economy.</p>
  - question: >-
      Expanding indigenous tonnage is only one part of the challenge. Vessels
      must also be maintained, repaired and operated efficiently, while local
      shipbuilding and marine engineering capabilities can create opportunities
      for wider industrial development. Where should Nigeria prioritise
      investment across vessel financing, shipbuilding, ship repair and marine
      services, and which of these areas offers the most realistic opportunities
      for private sector participation in the near to medium term?
    answer: >-
      <p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">&nbsp;</span></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">Ship repair is where money and
      skills can

      be developed, while shipbuilding shows whether the wider ecosystem is
      working.

      We should prioritise ship repair first and then build towards
      shipbuilding.</span></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">Vessels need to be dry docked and

      maintained. When we do not have adequate local facilities, Nigerian
      vessels go

      to places such as Takoradi in Ghana and Namibia for repairs. The money we
      spend

      abroad could support local businesses and create jobs for marine
      engineers,

      welders, electricians and other skilled workers.<o:p></o:p></span></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">I recall that <a
      href="https://www.primeaxisinnovations.com.ng/profiles/nigerian-maritime-administration-and-safety-agency-nimasa"
      target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">NIMASA </a>issued a marine
      notice

      around 2019 relating to local construction under the Cabotage regime,
      covering

      certain types of vessels such as tugboats, barges, anchor handling tugs
      and

      crew boats. We need to examine such initiatives and understand how they
      can

      support local capacity.<o:p></o:p></span></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">&nbsp;</span>We can learn from
      countries such as

      Indonesia, Japan, South Korea and China. Building a ship repair and

      shipbuilding industry requires more than a yard. It requires steel
      suppliers,

      certified welders, marine electricians, engine dealers, surveyors,
      classification

      services and reliable spare parts logistics.</p><p class="MsoNormal"><span
      lang="EN-US"><br></span></p><p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">We can
      start small with tugboats, barges

      and crew boats. We should develop capacity in phases and support local
      yards

      where they can deliver the required standards.<o:p></o:p></span></p><p
      class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">&nbsp;</span>We can also learn from
      local content

      development in the oil and gas industry, particularly the work of the
      Nigerian

      Content Development and Monitoring Board. There may be opportunities to
      extend

      similar thinking to selected vessel types and repair services.</p><p
      class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">&nbsp;</span>We must also consider
      the cost of energy

      and operations. These are important factors in the competitiveness of

      shipyards.</p><p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">&nbsp;</span>The
      objective is to build an ecosystem for

      repair, construction and eventually shipbuilding. When vessels are
      repaired

      abroad, we lose opportunities to develop local technical skills, retain

      spending and create industrial jobs. We should deliberately build these

      capabilities in Nigeria.</p>
  - question: >-
      Nigeria has pursued initiatives intended to strengthen indigenous
      shipping, including maritime financing mechanisms and policies designed to
      support local participation. However, the ultimate test is whether these
      interventions produce vessels in operation, sustainable shipping
      businesses and greater Nigerian participation in cargo movement. What
      practical changes would make the greatest difference in translating policy
      ambition into operational tonnage, and how should government and industry
      measure progress beyond announcements, allocations and programmes?
    answer: >-
      <p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">The sequence should be cargo
      assurance,

      then contracts, ship repair and shipbuilding.<o:p></o:p></span></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">&nbsp;</span>A ship is only
      operational when it is

      employed, has a contract, is insured, has a crew, is classed and earns
      more

      than its debt service. This applies whether the vessel was financed
      through the

      CVFF or another maritime loan.</p>


      <p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">&nbsp;</span>The pipeline against
      which our policies

      should be measured is important. Does a vessel have a contract? How does
      the

      operator intend to repay the loan? What opportunities are in the pipeline?
      We

      also need to match the vessel to the type of funding. Do we have the right

      vessel for the trade? What is its age and size? Is it suitable for the
      market

      it intends to serve?</p>


      <p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">&nbsp;</span>While efforts are
      being made to provide

      funding and acquire vessels, we must also examine the financing structure.
      What

      equity has been invested? How much debt has been approved? How much has

      actually been disbursed? We must move beyond approvals to track
      disbursement

      and results.</p>


      <p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">&nbsp;</span>How is the vessel
      employed? What is its

      utilisation rate? What is the debt service ratio for the quarter, or the
      debt

      service coverage ratio? How is this being reported?</p>


      <p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">&nbsp;</span></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">Failures often occur at financial
      close or

      when cargo has not been secured. Many policies focus on vessel
      registration but

      do not continue to examine how the vessel earns revenue. It is not enough
      to

      say we have registered a certain number of ships. We need to know how many
      are

      employed in gainful service, the duration of their contracts and their
      capacity

      to repay their loans.<o:p></o:p></span></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">&nbsp;</span>For a new operator, I
      would say that many

      Nigerian indigenous participants may not have the financial capacity to
      buy new

      vessels. Why not acquire good second hand vessels that are classed and

      surveyed, prove that you can deliver on contracts, and then expand over
      time?

      We should not become overly ambitious before demonstrating that we can
      perform.</p>


      <p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">&nbsp;</span>We should publish
      statistics on Nigerian

      registered vessels that are idle or not classed. This will help us measure
      what

      is happening in the industry.</p>


      <p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">&nbsp;</span>Another important
      issue is currency. If you

      earn in naira but take a loan in dollars, that is not viable without a way
      to

      manage the exchange rate risk. You can end up in a currency trap.</p>


      <p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">&nbsp;</span>Contracts and
      financing must match in

      currency, or the operator must have a credible way to manage the risk. We
      need

      to examine this practically.</p>


      <p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">&nbsp;</span>I read about Brazil’s
      Merchant Marine Fund,

      the Fundo da Marinha Mercante. We should study how its financing
      arrangements

      work and what lessons Nigeria can draw from them. I have also heard that

      Bangladesh increased its ocean going fleet from 36 vessels in 2018 to more
      than

      100 within about five years, with tax incentives for imported ships. These
      are

      examples worth examining to understand how tax treatment can support
      indigenous

      shipping capacity.</p>

      <p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">&nbsp;</span>We want to engage

      <p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">&nbsp;</span>We see this as an
      opportunity to solve a

      national problem. A Nigerian exporter who needs to move cargo to Europe
      should

      not be left entirely at the mercy of foreign shipping companies.</p>


      <p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">&nbsp;</span>&nbsp;Agricultural
      produce, for example, is time

      sensitive. When it is delayed and reaches the end user in poor condition,
      its

      quality may have deteriorated.</p>


      <p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">&nbsp;</span>We want to help fill

  - question: >-
      Nigeria’s participation in global shipping requires more than well
      intentioned policies and financing initiatives. It demands deliberate
      steps to translate policy into operational capacity, attract sustained
      private investment, expand indigenous fleet ownership and enable Nigerian
      shipping companies to compete in international markets. From your
      perspective, what practical actions must government and the private sector
      take, individually and collectively, to move Nigeria from policy ambition
      to measurable participation in global shipping, and what priorities should
      guide this effort over the next five to ten years?
    answer: >-
      <p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">To move from policy to practice,
      we must

      take deliberate steps and be open with ourselves.<o:p></o:p></span></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">&nbsp;</span>Data

      is very important. Let us publish baseline data on Nigerian controlled
      ships

      that are actually trading. If companies have applied for maritime funding,
      we

      should publish the figures.</p>


      <p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">&nbsp;If

      there have been disbursements, let us make them known. If there are
      impediments

      to disbursement, those should also be made public.<o:p></o:p></span></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">&nbsp;</span>Freight paid abroad
      represents a leakage

      from the Nigerian economy. Dry docking vessels abroad is another leakage.
      We

      need to identify the cargo available for international shipping in
      Nigeria, the

      major cargo owners, the routes and volumes, and who makes the freight

      decisions.</p>


      <p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">&nbsp;</span>We

      should then link these cargoes to Nigerian operators.</p>


      <p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">&nbsp;</span>Government should be
      an anchor for

      government cargo. We should identify state owned and government linked
      trade

      where Nigeria can nominate the carrier.</p>


      <p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">&nbsp;</span>Most of our exports
      are sold on a Free on

      Board (FOB) basis, whereas other countries may use Cost, Insurance and
      Freight

      (CIF) arrangements.</p>


      <p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">&nbsp;</span>The

      terms determine who arranges and pays for shipping. We need to examine
      where

      changes are commercially appropriate and how they could help develop
      Nigerian

      tonnage and retain more freight revenue in the country.</p>


      <p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">&nbsp;</span>We should open up
      suitable government cargo

      opportunities to qualified Nigerian operators, whether public or private.
      Let

      us publish baseline data showing how many Nigerian controlled ships are

      trading. If the CVFF window has been opened, let us publish whether there
      have

      been disbursements and explain the process. I heard that 99 people had
      applied

      for the CVFF. Has there been any disbursement? What are the impediments?
      What

      are the grey areas that need to be addressed? We need transparency so that
      we can

      measure and scale what works.</p>


      <p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">&nbsp;</span>Nigeria’s election to
      Category C of the

      International Maritime Organization (IMO) Council was an achievement. But
      that

      achievement must be backed by action. The IMO will eventually ask what
      Nigeria

      has achieved.</p>


      <p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">&nbsp;</span>&nbsp;<b>Where are
      the ships? What capacity have we

      built?</b></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">&nbsp;</span>We must build tonnage
      by linking it to

      cargo. Government should work with cargo owners and open opportunities to

      qualified Nigerian operators. We can discuss national carrier status, but
      we

      must not put the cart before the horse.</p>


      <p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">&nbsp;</span>We

      should not say, “Find a vessel first, and then we will find cargo for
      you.” It

      does not work that way. Let us identify the cargo first, then identify the

      Nigerian operators capable of providing vessels for it.</p>


      <p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">We should also make domestic ship
      repair

      the default where the necessary capacity and standards exist. When you
      have the

      ship and the cargo, you must have a place to repair the
      vessel.<o:p></o:p></span></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">&nbsp;</span>As I

      have said, it is an ecosystem. We must build it and create momentum. We
      must

      address port delays, inconsistencies and other gaps that affect
      operations.</p>


      <p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">Indigenous capacity should help
      retain

      freight revenue in Nigeria. Ship repair should build technical strength,
      while

      shipbuilding should extend into manufacturing, engineering and industrial
      jobs.<o:p></o:p></span></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">&nbsp;</span>Government’s role is
      to grow the cargo

      pipeline, help build credibility around financing and establish a well run

      pilot programme. The private sector must perform when given the
      opportunity and

      demonstrate a business model that deserves to be scaled.</p>
