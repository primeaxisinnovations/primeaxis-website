      together a

      policy. It is really about implementation. When you think about the
      maritime

      wealth of the nation, a significant proportion of that activity sits in
      Lagos.

      That is why it is important to have the right enabling environment,
      whether in

      water transportation, port development, fisheries, coastal tourism or
      coastal

      communities. All of these areas are very important.<o:p></o:p></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal"><o:p>&nbsp;</o:p></p>

      fisheries. There is a lot of illegal, unregulated and unreported fishing

      generator

      for not just the state but the nation. I believe a lot more can be done in
      this

      area.<o:p></o:p></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal"><o:p>&nbsp;</o:p></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">I also believe local tourism provides significant
      value. It

      is not only about economic revenue. It also creates opportunities for

      people to participate in the maritime ecosystem and gain
      employment.<o:p></o:p></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal"><o:p>&nbsp;</o:p></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">Water transportation then becomes an enabler for
      these

      sectors. Transportation is something that is often subsidised because it
      is an

      enabler for the economy and helps other parts of the economy to
      grow.<o:p></o:p></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal"><o:p>&nbsp;</o:p></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">Passenger transport will usually require some level
      of

      support, but when you think about cargo transport, that is where the state
      can

      potentially generate revenue beyond infrastructure, where the
      infrastructure

      exists.<o:p></o:p></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal"><o:p>&nbsp;</o:p></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">Cargo currently falls within the remit of the
      Nigerian Ports

      Authority, and Lagos State wants to work in tandem with the NPA. Lagos is
      not

      trying to take on federal duties because there are important
      jurisdictional

      issues. What we want to ensure is collaboration.<o:p></o:p></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal"><o:p>&nbsp;</o:p></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">As the ports operate within Lagos, we want to ensure
      that

      they adhere to safety measures while also creating an enabling environment
      for

      businesses to thrive. We have Lekki Deep Sea Port, Apapa and Tin Can
      Island.

      The question is how we ensure that these ports are properly integrated
      into the

      plans for Lagos State.<o:p></o:p></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal"><o:p>&nbsp;</o:p></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">So, that is where I currently see cargo sitting. The

      environment.

      and

      federal authorities is essential.<o:p></o:p></p>

  - question: >-
      The ambition for water transportation now goes beyond simply providing an
      alternative to road transport. With the push towards significantly higher
      passenger capacity, larger and more modern vessels, improved ferry
      terminals and projects such as Omi Èkó, what does the infrastructure and
      operating model for a genuinely scaled Lagos water transport system look
      like, and how should it connect with the state road and rail networks to
      function as one mobility system?
    answer: >-
      <p class="MsoNormal">What you have described is exactly what Lagos State
      is

      building. Lagos no longer builds any mode of transportation in
      isolation.<o:p></o:p></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal"><o:p>&nbsp;</o:p></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">Connectivity is now part of the planning
      process.<o:p></o:p></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal"><o:p>&nbsp;</o:p></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">You are seeing that come into play at Marina, where
      there is

      a major interchange, and Lagos has plans to implement two major
      interchanges,

      at Marina and Mile 2, bringing together bus, rail and water
      transportation.<o:p></o:p></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">Looking closely at water transportation, Omi Èkó is
      set to

      deliver vessels with passenger capacities ranging from 50 to 200
      passengers.

      The whole idea is to significantly increase the number of people using
      water

      transportation.<o:p></o:p></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">There are significant advantages to water
      transportation,

      particularly time savings. The vessels being procured are also expected to

      reduce carbon emissions. That has implications not just for individual

      passengers but for livelihoods and businesses because time savings also

      translate into economic benefits.<o:p></o:p></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal"><o:p>&nbsp;</o:p></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">However, we also have to think about what happens
      after

      people get off the ferry. How do they reach their final destination? That
      is

      where first-mile and last-mile connectivity becomes
      important.<o:p></o:p></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal"><o:p>&nbsp;</o:p></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">Lagos has first-mile and last-mile bus services that
      can

      connect passengers to the BRT network where it is available, particularly
      along

      major routes.<o:p></o:p></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal"><o:p>&nbsp;</o:p></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">The way Lagos looks at it is that road and water

      transportation must work in tandem, with rail also connected where it is

      available. The good thing is that there is one payment system through the
      Cowry

      card, which is used across road, rail and water
      transportation.<o:p></o:p></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal"><o:p>&nbsp;</o:p></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">At the moment, the main connections are between road
      and

      water transportation. Lagos has also prioritised roads leading to major

      jetties. The <b>Lagos State Government</b>, through the Ministry of Works,
      works with

      the Ministry of Transportation to prioritise roads leading to jetties
      because,

      ultimately, the objective is to aid mobility across the
      state.<o:p></o:p></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal"><o:p>&nbsp;</o:p></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">It is working, but there is still a lot more to be
      done,

      particularly as we look to revamp terminals and jetties.<o:p></o:p></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal"><o:p>&nbsp;</o:p></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">We are also looking at park-and-ride facilities. The
      future

      of the water transportation system we are building is one where people can
      park

      their cars and continue their journey by water.<o:p></o:p></p>

      <p class="MsoNormal"><o:p>&nbsp;</o:p></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">When we think about electric transportation, we think
      about

      sustainability.&nbsp;</p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">Lagos State has experience with electrification
      through

      other transport modes. The Blue Line, for example, is fully electrified,

      think

      about the requirements for water transportation.<o:p></o:p></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal"><o:p>&nbsp;</o:p></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">The same principle has to apply to water

      have to make sure that the different modes have access to adequate

      infrastructure and reliable power.<o:p></o:p></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">The <b><a href="#" target="_blank" rel="noopener
      noreferrer">Omi Èkó project</a></b> comes with 12 terminals that are
      fitted

      with charging infrastructure. The next requirement is ensuring that
      adequate

      power gets to each of these jetties so that the charging infrastructure
      can

      operate effectively.<o:p></o:p></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal"><o:p>&nbsp;</o:p></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">For electric water transportation to work at scale,

      therefore, it is not enough to procure electric vessels.<o:p></o:p></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal"><o:p>&nbsp;</o:p></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">The supporting infrastructure has to be in place,
      including

      charging facilities and adequate power at the terminals.<o:p></o:p></p>
  - question: >-
      Safety will ultimately determine whether increased capacity translates

      across vessel standards, operator professionalism, enforcement, emergency
      response, service reliability and the overall passenger experience? And
      how can the transition to higher standards bring the existing informal and
      smaller operators into the modern system rather than simply displace them?
    answer: >-
      <p class="MsoNormal">What Lagos does is learn. We have a lot of
      peer-to-peer

      learning, and the good thing is that we have other transport modes that
      have

      gone through similar transitions.<o:p></o:p></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal"><o:p>&nbsp;</o:p></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">When those systems were being implemented, there were

      challenges. We look at what has worked and what has not worked, and we

      replicate the approaches that are relevant to the
      waterways.<o:p></o:p></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal"><o:p>&nbsp;</o:p></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">There is something called the Bus Industry Transition
      Plan.

      This is where you have seen Danfo buses being phased out in tandem with
      their

      unions and replaced with more standard buses.<o:p></o:p></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal"><o:p>&nbsp;</o:p></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">A similar process is happening on the waterways
      through the

      Vessel Industry Transition Plan, or VITP. The objective is to help
      formalise

      the informal sector rather than simply remove them from the
      system.<o:p></o:p></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal"><o:p>&nbsp;</o:p></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">They will be given options. One option is to come
      into the

      new system as part of consortiums that will operate the boats being
      purchased.

      Another option is a buyout plan. A third option is to move to other feeder

      routes, with some form of compensation.<o:p></o:p></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal"><o:p>&nbsp;</o:p></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">The principle is that the project should not simply
      displace

      people who have depended on the waterways for their
      livelihoods.<o:p></o:p></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal"><o:p>&nbsp;</o:p></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">Our foreign partners have also made it clear that no

      Lagosian should lose their source of livelihood because of a new
      project.<o:p></o:p></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">Local knowledge is also extremely important. Even
      where

      international experience is brought in, you still need local knowledge
      because,

      at the end of the day, it is water. You must have people who understand
      the

      local waterways and the environment in which the system
      operates.<o:p></o:p></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal"><o:p>&nbsp;</o:p></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">So, there is a detailed plan behind the transition,
      with

      significant stakeholder engagement and consultation. The objective is to
      ensure

      that existing operators are not simply phased out without a solid process
      for

      managing the transition.<o:p></o:p></p>
