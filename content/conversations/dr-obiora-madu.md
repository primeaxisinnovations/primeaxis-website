  - question: >-
      Africa has created an ambitious framework for continental trade, but the
      systems that support that trade remain largely fragmented along national
      lines. What fundamental shift in the way Africa thinks about trade,
      logistics and supply chains is required if we are to move from a
      collection of national markets to a genuinely integrated continental
      economy?
    answer: >-
      <p class="MsoNormal">Thank you very much.</p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">My response will be that there is a need for a
      fundamental

      shift in the way Africa thinks about trade.</p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">Africa should stop thinking about trade as a
      collection of

      national activities and start thinking of it as continental value
      creation. The

      reason is that strategies developed by individual nations may not
      necessarily

      coordinate to deliver the benefits that are expected.</p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">We have spent decades building economies around
      borders.

      Trucks cannot move freely to deliver goods. Agreements do not move goods;

      supply chains do.</p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">We need to standardise documentation, procedures and,
      in

      some cases, even the way agencies operate to make the system work.</p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">What we have achieved so far is not full integration.
      I

      think the agreement has provided the basis for integration, but the real
      work

      is to make the architecture operational. That is why not much has
      happened.</p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">If cocoa is produced in Ghana, processed in Côte
      d'Ivoire,

      packaged in Nigeria and sold across several African countries, we should
      not

      see four separate national supply chains. We should see one African supply

      chain.</p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">So, my position is simple: Africa should begin
      designing

      supply chains without allowing national borders to define the
      architecture.</p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">We must move from asking, “How do we protect what is
      within

      our borders?” to asking, “How do we create more value within Africa?”</p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">The mindset must shift.<o:p></o:p></p>
  - question: >-
      Trade corridors are often discussed in terms of roads, railways, ports and
      border infrastructure. But a successful trade corridor is ultimately an
      economic ecosystem. What separates a transport route from a truly
      productive trade corridor, and what must exist around these corridors for
      them to generate investment, manufacturing, employment and sustained
      intra-African trade?
    answer: >-
      <p class="MsoNormal">A road is infrastructure. A corridor is an economic
      system.</p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">We must always see the distinction between the two
      because

      it is very important.<o:p></o:p></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">A road will allow trucks to move, but a productive
      trade

      corridor will involve goods, capital, information, skills and investment
      moving

      efficiently and predictably.<o:p></o:p></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">I guess that is why we are having challenges with
      many of

      our corridors.<o:p></o:p></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">For any corridor to become economically productive,
      five

      things must come together: connectivity, trade facilitation, productive

      capacity, logistics services and market access.<o:p></o:p></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">These are very critical.</p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">A corridor without production is simply a transport
      route. A

      corridor with production, logistics, finance, technology, skills and
      market

      access becomes an economic artery.</p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">That is what all our corridors must become if AfCFTA
      is

      really going to take its place.</p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">We should stop measuring corridors simply by
      kilometres

      because that is what I see happening now.&nbsp;</p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">We have to measure them by the trade created,
      businesses

      established and generated, manufacturing stimulated, logistics costs
      reduced

      and value retained for Africa.</p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">Trade corridors are key to AfCFTA's success, but
      again, almost

      everything we are talking about requires a mindset shift. When we define a

      trade corridor correctly, we can then begin working in that
      direction.<o:p></o:p></p>
  - question: >-
      Africa continues to invest in infrastructure, yet the cost and
      unpredictability of moving goods remain major constraints on
      competitiveness.

      Why does infrastructure investment so often fail to translate into
      corresponding improvements in logistics performance, and where should
      policymakers look beyond physical infrastructure for the real sources of
      inefficiency?
    answer: >-
      <p class="MsoNormal">Infrastructure is a very necessary tool for logistics
      to

      prosper, but infrastructure alone does not create logistics
      performance.&nbsp;</p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">You can build beautiful ports, construct good roads
      and

      still have a dysfunctional port and transport system.</p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">Building new highways while trucks continue to spend
      hours

      at checkpoints does not make sense. You can modernise a border post and
      still

      have mountains of paper based processes creating delays.</p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">So, the real issue is that logistics performance is a

      function of an entire system; it is not a matter of roads alone.</p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">Why are we not seeing corresponding improvements
      despite the

      investments being made in infrastructure?</p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">The difference between hardware and operating
      architecture

      is where the challenge is coming from.</p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">Africa has invested significantly in hardware. What
      we have

      not invested sufficiently in is the operating architecture around that

      hardware.<o:p></o:p></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">In fact, in some cases, most of those investments are
      not

      real.</p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">We need to examine customs processes, regulatory

      duplication, corruption, informal payments, poor information, fragmented

      government agencies, inefficient truck utilisation and poor warehousing

      systems.<o:p></o:p></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">We must also address the soft infrastructure skills,

      inadequate supply chain visibility and the broader capability gaps within
      the

      system.</p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">Then there is the predictability
      challenge.<o:p></o:p></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">Disruption has become a permanent feature of African
      supply

      chains.</p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">Unnecessary delays and port congestion continue to
      undermine

      efficiency.</p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">Policymakers should move beyond asking how much

      infrastructure we have built and start asking:<o:p></o:p></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">How efficiently does the entire system convert

      infrastructure into economic value?</p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">That is what it means for the people.<o:p></o:p></p>
  - question: >-
      Digitalisation is rapidly changing customs, ports, payments, documentation
      and supply chain management. But digital tools can also reproduce the
      weaknesses of the systems they are built upon. What would a genuinely
      intelligent African digital trade ecosystem look like, and what must
      governments and businesses do differently to ensure technology removes
      friction rather than merely digitising it?
    answer: >-
      <p class="MsoNormal">Digitisation is critical, but you cannot digitise
      what is

      not in existence.</p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">You have to have processes before you automate or
      digitise

      them, and it is useless to digitise a bad process.</p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">So, what do you do?<o:p></o:p></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">You redesign it before you digitise it.</p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">If you take a dysfunctional paper process and put it
      on a

      computer, you have not created digital transformation. You have simply
      created

      electronic bureaucracy.</p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">Our trade ecosystem should allow information to move
      across

      borders as easily as the goods themselves.</p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">We need interoperability. For that to happen, we need
      single

      sources of good, common data standards, digital identities, interoperable

      payment systems and electronic documentation.<o:p></o:p></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">Most importantly, real cargo visibility and
      risk-based

      regulation are key.</p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">Artificial intelligence can do a lot for us in this

      particular direction, but technology is not the only part of the
      equation.</p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">Government must be willing to redesign regulations
      around

      digital processes rather than simply adding technology to old
      bureaucracy.</p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">The ultimate objective should not be to ask, “How
      digital

      are our ports?”<o:p></o:p></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">The question should be:<o:p></o:p></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">“How much friction has the digital ecosystem removed
      from

      Africa's trade?”<o:p></o:p></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">That should be the metric.<o:p></o:p></p>
  - question: >-
      Infrastructure can be financed. Technology can be acquired. But capability
      has to be built. From your experience developing supply chain
      professionals across Africa, where is the continent's greatest capability
      deficit today, and what kind of human capital will Africa need to manage
      increasingly sophisticated regional and global supply chains?
    answer: >-
      <p class="MsoNormal">Africa is not short of talented
      people.<o:p></o:p></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">Our greatest deficit is the shortage of people who
      can

      connect strategy, technology, operations, finance and execution.</p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">A supply chain professional of the future cannot be
      someone

      who only understands procurement, warehousing or transportation.</p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">We need systems thinkers on the continent people who

      understand the entire value chain.<o:p></o:p></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">Supply chain professionals are beginning to make more

      progress because they have started to understand finance, negotiate

      internationally, manage technology, understand trade policies, anticipate
      risk

      and lead.</p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">We need a major shift from theoretical learning to
      applied

      capability.</p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">That is where the RAPID Supply Chain Framework,
      designed by

      Multimix Academy, comes in.</p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">You have to subject learning to real practical
      situations.

      That is where simulations come in.<o:p></o:p></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">The War Room is basically like a pressure-cooker
      situation

      where people face real-life scenarios and have to make decisions. Whatever
      you

      choose will have implications for your KPIs.</p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">That is why I strongly believe that War Room
      thinking,

      scenario-based planning and RAPID will be part of the way out for
      Africa.</p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">Because disruption has become permanent, I think the
      next

      generation of supply chain leaders in Africa must be willing to make
      decisions

      under heavy pressure.&nbsp;</p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">They must be ready to lead.<o:p></o:p></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">Africa does not need simply better supply-chain
      managers.</p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">Africa needs supply chain leaders who can influence

      government, financiers and policymakers.</p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">This is the capability gap we must close and rapidly
      too.<o:p></o:p></p>
  - question: >-
      Logistics is often treated as a support function for trade. But could it
      be much more than that? How can Africa deliberately use logistics and
      supply chain strategy to drive industrialisation connecting agriculture,
      manufacturing, distribution and markets in ways that create more value
      within Africa rather than simply moving imported goods around the
      continent?
    answer: >-
      <p class="MsoNormal">I agree completely with the question about logistics
      and

      supply chains driving industrialisation. In fact, I will add that
      logistics

      should be one of Africa's industrialisation strategies.</p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">We have treated logistics as a cost of doing
      business.<o:p></o:p></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">We should begin to treat logistics as an enabler of
      economic

      transformation.</p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">If you look at agriculture, for example, Africa
      should stop

      exporting raw agricultural commodities.</p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">Each time you ship raw commodities out, you ship
      employment

      with them, and we eventually import finished products made from those same

      commodities that we exported.</p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">Quite an irony.</p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">Supply chains should connect the farmer to
      aggregation,

      packaging, cold chain systems and transportation. These are all the things
      we

      need.<o:p></o:p></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">We know it cannot happen in one day, but we want to
      start

      seeing evidence that it is working.<o:p></o:p></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">The same principles apply to minerals and other areas
      where

      Africa has strength.</p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">What we have to do is call for value chain
      orchestration.</p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">Government must create the policy
      environment.<o:p></o:p></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">Infrastructure must connect production centres to
      markets.</p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">Financial institutions must provide
      capital.<o:p></o:p></p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">Logistics providers must create reliable movement and

      storage systems.</p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">Manufacturers need to do better, and more
      manufacturers need

      to emerge.</p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">So the objective is not simply to move more African
      goods.</p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">The objective is to create African value before those
      goods

      reach the market.</p>


      <p class="MsoNormal">That is what it means for logistics to become the
      engine of

      industrialisation in Africa.<o:p></o:p></p>
