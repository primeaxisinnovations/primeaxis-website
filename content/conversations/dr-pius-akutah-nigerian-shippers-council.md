qa:
  - question: >-
      Cargo clearance times at Nigerian ports remain a competitive disadvantage
      for shippers. What concrete progress has the Council made, and where does
      the work remain unfinished?
    answer: >-
      <p>We have seen measurable improvement in container dwell times at Apapa
      in recent periods, driven primarily by the electronic call-up system and
      improved pre-arrival documentation protocols the Council has been
      instrumental in advancing. Where we are today, however, is still not a
      competitive position — leading ports in Asia clear cargo in a fraction of
      the time — and Nigerian traders absorb that differential across every
      import and export transaction. Our current focus is advancing pre-arrival
      documentation filing as standard practice — targeting submission well
      ahead of vessel arrival — so that customs assessment begins before cargo
      reaches the terminal. The coordination required between the Council,
      Nigeria Customs Service, and the Nigerian Ports Authority to deliver this
      consistently remains our primary implementation bottleneck.</p>
  - question: >-
      The Coastal and Inland Shipping (Cabotage) Act has been in place for over
      two decades with limited impact on domestic vessel ownership. What
      amendments does the Council consider most critical?
    answer: >-
      <p>The Act's waivers mechanism has effectively become the instrument
      through which its intent is systematically circumvented — foreign vessels
      routinely secure waivers to trade on cabotage routes because qualifying
      Nigerian tonnage simply does not exist to fill the gap. Breaking this
      cycle requires simultaneous action on two fronts: tightening waiver
      criteria and deploying the Cabotage Vessel Financing Fund at meaningful
      scale to enable Nigerian operators to actually acquire compliant vessels.
      The CVFF has been collected but not disbursed at the volumes the sector
      requires, and that is the central structural failure. The amendments we
      are advocating introduce mandatory CVFF deployment timelines and sunset
      provisions on waivers that create genuine commercial incentives for
      domestic shipowners rather than perpetual foreign substitution.</p>
  - question: >-
      There has been longstanding discussion about the coordination dynamic
      between the Nigerian Shippers' Council and NIMASA. How would you
      characterise the current working relationship?
    answer: >-
      <p>The challenge is institutional rather than personal — both agencies
      have legitimate mandates that intersect at specific points in the maritime
      value chain, and we have not historically had the formal protocols to
      manage those intersections efficiently. When one agency issues a levy
      circular without consulting the other, the shipper is the one absorbing
      the cost of that misalignment. We are working through the Federal Ministry
      of Marine and Blue Economy to establish a structured inter-agency
      coordination framework with defined consultation requirements before
      policy decisions affecting cargo owners are implemented. Progress is real,
      but converting framework goodwill into operational protocols takes
      sustained effort on both sides.</p>
  - question: >-
      The shipper has historically been a less powerful policy voice relative to
      shipping lines and terminal operators. How is that changing?
    answer: >-
      <p>The shipper is the originating economic actor in the maritime chain —
      without cargo owners, there are no ships, no terminals, no freight
      forwarders — yet shippers have historically held policy influence
      disproportionately low relative to that role. The Council's function is to
      aggregate a diffuse constituency and give it a coherent, evidence-based
      voice at every forum where maritime policy is made. A significant part of
      our current advocacy focuses on freight rate transparency — pushing for
      shipping lines calling Nigerian ports to make their rate structures
      accessible to cargo owners, a basic information right that has
      historically been denied to African shippers. We are also extending our
      advocacy into demurrage dispute resolution, where the structural
      information asymmetry between global shipping lines and Nigerian importers
      produces significant and largely hidden costs.</p>
  - question: >-
      Where do you want to see the Nigerian Shippers' Council in five years, and
      what would that success look like?
    answer: >-
      <p>I want the Council to be Africa's leading model for cargo owner
      advocacy — resolving freight disputes in days rather than months,
      publishing real-time maritime intelligence that genuinely informs
      commercial decisions, and demonstrably reducing the freight cost premium
      Nigerian shippers pay relative to comparable markets. That premium is
      real, measurable, and significant, and closing it would be the most direct
      contribution any maritime institution could make to Nigerian export
      competitiveness right now. Achieving it will require deeper technical
      capacity, better data systems, and — frankly — a willingness to take
      positions that challenge well-resourced commercial interests when the
      evidence demands it.</p>
