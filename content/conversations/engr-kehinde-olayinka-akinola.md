  Since assuming office as Chairman of the Governing Council on 3 October 2024,
  Engr. Kehinde Olayinka Akinola has been involved in advancing the

  - question: >-
      Since assuming office as Chairman of the Governing Council of the Maritime
      Academy of Nigeria, what achievements at the Academy are dearest to you,
      and what would you consider your most significant contributions?
    answer: >-
      <p style="margin: 0px 0px 12px;">One of the things that stood out to us
      when we assumed office was the state of electricity supply at the Academy.
      After our inauguration and initial meeting, we visited the campus and
      discovered that there was no public electricity supply. The line from Eket
      to Oron had been disconnected for about two to three years because of a
      disagreement, and the Academy had been relying on generators.</p><p
      style="margin: 0px 0px 12px;">We immediately considered how to address the
      situation. The Council made provision for solar power to support the
      residences and offices, while we also pursued the restoration of the
      public electricity supply from Eket to Oron. This was important not only
      for the Academy but also for the host community.</p><p style="margin: 0px
      0px 12px;">Today, the public electricity supply has been restored. There
      was great jubilation when power returned, and the Academy now enjoys
      electricity around the clock. This has made a significant difference,
      particularly for cadets who need to study at night.</p><p style="margin:
      0px 0px 12px;">We have also worked towards upgrading the Academy’s
      training facilities. The smart boards and simulators are now operational,
      and these improvements are helping to strengthen the quality of
      instruction and practical training.</p><p style="margin: 0px 0px
      12px;">When you improve the facilities and create a better learning
      environment, you are also able to attract good instructors. The quality of
      training improves, and the students become better prepared for the
      industry.</p><p style="margin: 0px 0px 12px;">We are beginning to see the
      benefits in the opportunities available to our graduates. In 2025, more
      than 80 cadets secured ship placements for their sea-time training on
      their graduation day. In 2026, NLNG alone took 50 cadets, while other
      shipowners are also providing opportunities.</p><p style="margin: 0px 0px
      12px;">Our aspiration is to make the Maritime Academy of Nigeria a leading
      world-class institution for cadet and maritime professional
      training.</p><p style="margin: 0px 0px 12px;">&nbsp;Beyond the cadet
      programme, more than 4,000 people attend various short courses at the
      Academy annually. We want to continue improving the institution’s capacity
      to serve the maritime industry.</p>
  - question: >-
      What are the major priorities of your Council’s five-year development
      plan, and how are initiatives such as the proposed training vessel and
      international partnerships progressing towards implementation?
    answer: >-
      <p>Our five-year development plan is designed around sustainability. We do
      not want a situation where the Academy makes progress during one
      administration or period and then experiences a decline when there is a
      change in leadership. We want to establish structures and initiatives that
      will sustain the institution’s development over time.</p><p>One of the
      major projects in the plan is the proposed training vessel. We are looking
      at having this in place within the third year of our tenure. The vessel
      will provide cadets with practical exposure and an opportunity to
      understand shipboard operations. However, we must recognise that one
      training vessel cannot provide all the sea-time required by every
      cadet.</p><p><p>&nbsp;We therefore need other partnerships and
      opportunities within the industry.</p><p>We have also been working on
      international partnerships. One of the countries we have engaged is
      Liberia. Liberia has the largest registered fleet in the world, and we saw
      an opportunity to establish a relationship that would benefit our cadets
      and the Academy.</p><p>We visited Liberia, and subsequently, a delegation
      from Liberia also visited the Academy. We have agreed in principle to
      develop a Memorandum of Understanding that will provide for the exchange
      of lecturers, faculty members and students. It will also create
      opportunities for Nigerian cadets to undertake training aboard ships
      flying the Liberian flag.</p><p>Our target is to conclude and sign the
      agreement by December 2026.</p><p>We have also visited Morocco to explore
      areas of bilateral cooperation. We expect further engagement towards
      developing that relationship.</p><p>Another important development is the
      certification process involving the Standards Organisation of Nigeria. The
      Academy underwent an external audit and received certification following
      the process, which was carried out in two stages. The Director-General of
      SON personally presented the certification.</p><p>All these initiatives
      are directed at strengthening the Academy, improving access to sea-time,
      exposing our cadets to international opportunities and ensuring that the
      training we provide is recognised beyond Nigeria.</p><p><br></p></p>
  - question: >-
      Access to mandatory sea-time remains a major challenge for Nigerian cadets
      seeking to complete their training and progress towards professional
      certification. Beyond the proposed training vessel and international
      partnerships, what sustainable measures is the Academy pursuing with
      NIMASA, shipowners and other industry stakeholders to expand sea-time
      placements and improve employment opportunities for its graduates?
    answer: >-
      <p>Sea-time is a very important part of maritime training. A cadet may
      receive good classroom instruction and practical training in the Academy,
      but there is a stage at which the cadet must go to sea and gain the
      required experience before progressing towards professional
      certification.</p><p>That is why we are not relying on the proposed
      training vessel alone. As I have said, the vessel will help, but it cannot
      accommodate all our cadets or meet all their sea-time
      requirements.</p><p>Our proposed partnership with Liberia is one of the
      ways we intend to address this challenge. Through the relationship, our
      cadets will have opportunities to train aboard ships flying the Liberian
      flag. The exchange arrangement will also allow for the movement of
      students and lecturers between the institutions.</p><p>We are equally
      encouraged by the opportunities being provided by companies such as NLNG
      and other shipowners. In 2025, more than 80 cadets secured ship
      placements, while in 2026, NLNG alone took 50 cadets. These are
      encouraging developments, and we want to see more shipowners provide such
      opportunities.</p><p>The issue of sea-time requires cooperation. The
      Academy cannot solve it alone. We need the support of NIMASA, shipowners,
      maritime companies, international partners and other stakeholders. Our
      responsibility is to continue producing competent cadets and creating
      relationships that will give them access to the practical experience they
      need.</p><p>Ultimately, we want our graduates to have the opportunity to
      demonstrate their competence and build careers in the maritime industry,
      both in Nigeria and internationally.</p><p><br></p>
  - question: >-
      The maritime industry is undergoing significant changes driven by
      digitalisation, automation, decarbonisation and new vessel technologies.
       How is the Academy adapting its curriculum, training facilities and technical programmes to ensure that Nigerian cadets are equipped with the practical skills and knowledge required by the evolving global shipping industry?
    answer: >-
      <p>We recognise that the maritime industry is changing, and the Academy
      must change with it. We are reviewing and improving our curriculum to
      ensure that our training remains relevant to developments in the
      industry.</p><p style="margin: 0px 0px 12px;">One of the areas we are
      looking at is ship design.</p><p><p>&nbsp;We want our cadets to understand
      not just how ships operate but also the principles behind their design. We
      are particularly interested in preparing them for developments in cleaner
      energy, including the design of vessels that can operate on compressed
      natural gas rather than diesel, which is a more environmentally friendly
      option.</p><p>We are also developing a shipyard facility at the Academy.
      The facility is partially completed, and we intend to complete it so that
      vessels can be brought in for repairs. This will give cadets the
      opportunity to observe the repair process, participate where appropriate
      and learn directly from practical operations.</p><p>We are also looking at
      introducing artificial intelligence into our training programmes. We
      cannot afford to train our cadets using only what was relevant many years
      ago. We must keep abreast of developments and ensure that they understand
      the technologies shaping the industry.</p><p>Our cadets already have
      opportunities to develop practical skills through the facilities available
      at the Academy. They learn how to dismantle and reassemble engines,
      operate simulators and respond to emergencies, including shipboard fires.
      They also undergo survival training.</p><p>We have an electronic library
      with thousands of learning resources and publications, including materials
      from the International Maritime Organization. Our cadets have access to
      these resources to support their studies and broaden their understanding
      of the industry.</p><p>We are committed to creating an environment where
      cadets are motivated to learn and lecturers are encouraged to deliver
      quality instruction. The objective is to ensure that our graduates possess
      the technical knowledge and practical competence required to function
      effectively in the modern maritime industry.</p></p>
  - question: >-
      Following the May 2026 regional maritime education initiative involving
      institutions in Nigeria, Ghana and Côte d’Ivoire under the Maritime
      Organisation of West and Central Africa (MOWCA), what progress has been
      made towards curriculum harmonisation, institutional partnerships, cadet
      exchanges and the recognition of maritime qualifications across the
      region?
    answer: >-
      <p>I may not have the latest update on every aspect of that initiative,
      but I can speak about some of the engagements we have had with
      institutions in the region.</p><p style="margin: 0px 0px 12px;">The
      management of the Regional Maritime University in Ghana visited the
      Maritime Academy of Nigeria. They came to see our facilities and interact
      with us, and we held discussions on areas of cooperation. We are working
      towards developing a Memorandum of Understanding that will provide a
      framework for that relationship.</p><p>We also have a relationship with
      the Nigerian Maritime University at Okerenkoko. Students from the
      university come to the Maritime Academy for training.</p><p>These
      engagements are important because maritime education should not be viewed
      only within the boundaries of individual countries. The maritime industry
      is international, and our institutions should be able to collaborate,
      share experiences and develop the capacity of the region.</p><p>We want to
      see greater cooperation in areas such as student exchanges, faculty
      exchanges, curriculum development and the recognition of qualifications.
      If we can work together, our institutions will become stronger, and our
      graduates will have better opportunities across the region.</p><p>Our goal
      is to contribute to a stronger maritime education system in West and
      Central Africa, where institutions can learn from one another and prepare
      professionals who can serve the wider industry.</p>
  - question: >-
      You have strongly defended the Academy’s specialised maritime mandate
      against proposals to convert it into a conventional university. How can
      the institution expand its academic and research capacity, including the
      introduction of degree programmes, while preserving the specialised
      training, professional standards and international standing that define
      its purpose?
    answer: >-
      <p>I have had discussions with leaders of the National Assembly and the
      Minister of Education on this issue. My concern is that some of the people
      advocating for the conversion of the Academy into a conventional
      university may not fully understand the nature of professional maritime
      training.</p><p style="margin: 0px 0px 12px;">Maritime training is
      specialised. It is not enough to have academic qualifications without the
      technical competence and practical experience required to perform the
      job.</p><p><p>Let me use aviation as an example. If you want to employ a
      pilot, you are interested in the person’s ability to fly an aircraft and
      the technical competence required for the job. You are not simply looking
      for someone because they have a PhD. The same principle applies to
      maritime professionals. They must possess the technical skills and
      practical training required to work in the industry.</p><p>My concern is
      that if we focus too much on converting the Academy into a conventional
      university, we may lose the specialised technical training that makes the
      institution important.</p><p>There is also the issue of funding. If the
      Academy becomes a conventional university, it may come under the Ministry
      of Education and have to compete with existing federal universities for
      funding. At present, the Academy is under the Ministry of Marine and Blue
      Economy and receives funding through NIMASA. I believe this arrangement is
      more suitable for its specialised mandate.</p><p>However, I am not against
      the Academy expanding its academic capacity or introducing degree
      programmes. We have about 100 hectares of land available, and there is
      room for development.</p><p>We have engaged with stakeholders, and the
      National Assembly has taken a first reading on a proposed amendment to the
      law that would allow the institution to remain the Maritime Academy while
      also offering degree programmes.</p><p>What we want is an arrangement that
      allows us to expand academically without abandoning our core
      responsibility of professional maritime training. We can offer degree
      programmes while retaining the professional certificates and specialised
      technical training that define the Academy. The Nigerian Defence Academy
      provides an example of an institution that combines academic programmes
      with specialised professional training.</p><p>We must preserve what makes
      the Academy unique. Our focus should remain on producing competent
      maritime professionals who can meet the needs of the industry. We can
      expand, introduce new programmes and strengthen research, but we must not
      lose the practical and professional character of the institution.</p></p>
