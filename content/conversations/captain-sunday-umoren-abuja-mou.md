qa:
  - question: >-
      One of the core objectives of the Abuja MoU is strengthening maritime
      safety through effective Port State Control while supporting the
      development of competent maritime professionals across the region. A major
      concern for many Nigerian and African seafarers remains the international
      acceptability of their Certificates of Competency (CoCs). Without broader
      recognition, many qualified seafarers struggle to access international
      opportunities.

      What role is Abuja MoU playing in improving CoC recognition and creating
      greater mobility for seafarers within the region?
    answer: >-
      <p class="MsoNormal">The acceptability of Certificates of Competency is

      fundamentally a Flag State responsibility. Every member state of the

      International Maritime Organization (IMO) is responsible for ensuring that
      the

      certificates it issues comply with international standards and are
      recognised

      internationally.</p><p class="MsoNormal">I am aware that the
      Director-General of NIMASA and the

      Honourable Minister of Marine and Blue Economy are working diligently
      towards

      achieving wider international recognition of Nigeria's Certificates of

      Competency.<o:p></o:p></p><p class="MsoNormal"><o:p>&nbsp;</o:p>From the
      Abuja MoU’s perspective, our contribution is

      through the development of the Near Coastal Voyage (NCV) Code. The
      objective is

      to establish a framework that allows Certificates of Competency issued by

      member states to be mutually recognised for near-coastal operations within
      the

      Abuja MoU region.</p><p class="MsoNormal">Once fully adopted and
      implemented, the NCV Code will

      significantly improve regional labour mobility by enabling qualified
      seafarers

      to work across participating countries without unnecessary certification

      barriers. This will create employment opportunities, strengthen regional

      maritime capacity and build a more competitive African maritime
      workforce.<o:p></o:p></p><p class="MsoNormal">Regional cooperation in this
      area has significant potential

      to benefit both seafarers and maritime administrations.</p>
  - question: >-
      The eradication of substandard ships remains the core mandate of the Abuja
      MoU. Beyond Port State Control inspections, do current statistics show
      measurable progress in reducing substandard vessels?
    answer: >-
      <p class="MsoNormal">The eradication of substandard ships remains the
      primary

      mandate of the Abuja MoU regime. Every activity we undertake—capacity
      building,

      inspector training, technical cooperation, and harmonisation of inspection

      procedures—is directed toward this objective.<o:p></o:p></p><p
      class="MsoNormal"><o:p>&nbsp;</o:p>Yes, inspection data shows clear
      progress.</p><p class="MsoNormal">According to the Abuja MoU 2025 Annual
      Port State Inspection

      Data (APSID), 16 member authorities conducted 3,948 inspections,
      identifying

      1,571 deficiencies and resulting in 24 vessel detentions where serious

      deficiencies warranted enforcement action.</p><p class="MsoNormal">These
      figures reflect more than inspection activity. They

      demonstrate stronger regional participation, improved enforcement
      capacity, and

      increasing harmonisation of Port State Control procedures across West and

      Central Africa.<o:p></o:p></p><p class="MsoNormal"><o:p>&nbsp;</o:p>Today,
      all 22 eligible coastal states in the region are part

      of the Abuja MoU framework. The focus has therefore shifted from expansion
      to

      strengthening operational capacity. The strategic objective is for all
      member

      states to operate fully functional Port State Control regimes by
      2030.</p><p>


















      </p><p class="MsoNormal">Our goal is not simply to increase inspection
      numbers, but

      to build a uniform inspection regime where internationally accepted
      standards

      are applied consistently across all member authorities. As this system
      matures,

      it becomes increasingly difficult for substandard ships to operate within
      our

      waters.<o:p></o:p></p>
  - question: >-
      Foreign-flagged vessels account for a significant share of maritime trade
      across Africa, and marine pollution remains a persistent concern. What
      more can be done to reduce oil spills and pollution linked to shipping
      activities?
    answer: >-
      <p class="MsoNormal">The first line of defence is preventing substandard
      vessels

      from operating within our waters.<o:p></o:p></p><p
      class="MsoNormal"><o:p>&nbsp;</o:p>When ships are properly maintained,
      pollution-prevention

      equipment is functional and crews are adequately trained, the risk of
      pollution

      is significantly reduced.</p><p class="MsoNormal">Oil pollution generally
      falls into two categories:

      operational and accidental discharges.<o:p></o:p></p><p
      class="MsoNormal">Operational discharges are often deliberate, where
      operators

      illegally dispose of oily residues at sea to avoid disposal costs ashore.
      These

      actions constitute environmental crimes.</p><p
      class="MsoNormal">Accidental discharges result from equipment failure,
      poor

      maintenance or human error.<o:p></o:p></p><p class="MsoNormal">Port State
      Control plays a vital role in addressing this

      through inspections that verify compliance with the International
      Convention

      for the Prevention of Pollution from Ships (MARPOL).</p><p
      class="MsoNormal">However, inspections alone are not
      sufficient.<o:p></o:p></p><p class="MsoNormal">Maritime surveillance must
      be strengthened. Countries such

      as Singapore deploy advanced monitoring systems, including aircraft and

      helicopters capable of detecting oil slicks and tracing them back to

      responsible vessels.<o:p></o:p></p><p
      class="MsoNormal"><o:p>&nbsp;</o:p>Africa must increasingly adopt similar
      technologies. Many

      illegal discharges occur at night or involve vessels transiting without
      calling

      at ports. Without effective surveillance, detection is difficult.</p><p>






























      </p><p class="MsoNormal">When operators know they can be detected,
      tracked, and

      sanctioned, compliance improves significantly. Technology, therefore,
      serves as

      both a deterrent and an enforcement tool.<o:p></o:p></p>
  - question: >-
      Abuja MoU has steadily strengthened its regional profile. What is being
      done to position it among the world's leading Port State Control regimes?
    answer: >-
      <p class="MsoNormal">We are making steady progress toward positioning
      Abuja MoU

      among the leading Port State Control regimes globally.<o:p></o:p></p><p
      class="MsoNormal">The International Maritime Organization has acknowledged

      improvements within the region, although there remains room for further

      progress.<o:p></o:p></p><p class="MsoNormal"><o:p>&nbsp;</o:p>A key
      strategy is continuous review of operational

      procedures, drawing lessons from established regimes such as the Paris MoU

      while adapting best practices to West and Central Africa.</p><p
      class="MsoNormal"><br></p><p class="MsoNormal">Capacity building remains
      central, as knowledge is critical.</p><p class="MsoNormal">Participation
      in IMO meetings and technical forums is also

      essential, as these shape global maritime regulations. However, funding

      constraints limit participation for some member states.</p><p
      class="MsoNormal">To bridge this gap, Abuja MoU now provides structured

      briefings during its meetings, updating member states on IMO developments,

      including Port State Control and Flag State implementation
      issues.<o:p></o:p></p><p class="MsoNormal">We are also working with the
      IMO and development partners to

      strengthen technical cooperation and institutional capacity across member

      states.</p><p>


























      </p><p class="MsoNormal">Our ambition is clear: not only to adopt global
      best

      practices, but to become a pace-setter in maritime safety, Port State
      Control

      and regional maritime cooperation within the developing
      world.<o:p></o:p></p>
