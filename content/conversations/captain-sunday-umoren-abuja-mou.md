qa:
  - question: >-
      The Abuja MoU covers 22 member states with widely varying maritime
      administration capacity. How do you maintain consistent inspection
      standards across that diversity?
    answer: >-
      <p>Harmonisation through capacity investment is the only approach that
      produces results — mandating consistent standards without supporting the
      capacity to deliver them generates the appearance of compliance, not its
      substance. We have put significant numbers of port state control officers
      through the secretariat's training programme in recent years, and our
      shared regional database now gives any member state instant access to a
      vessel's full inspection history before it enters their waters. The
      variance in inspection quality across member states remains significant,
      and I will not pretend otherwise. But the states that received the most
      concentrated technical assistance in earlier cycles now consistently
      produce some of our strongest inspection outcomes, which tells us the
      investment model works.</p>
  - question: >-
      Detention rates in the region remain elevated relative to other MoU
      regions. What deficiency patterns concern you most, and what do they
      reveal about the fleet trading in African waters?
    answer: >-
      <p>Life-saving appliances, fire protection systems, and navigational
      equipment consistently account for the majority of serious deficiencies
      across our reporting cycles, and the persistence of this pattern is
      diagnostic. It tells us the fleet trading in African waters is
      disproportionately older tonnage operating on constrained margins, flagged
      under administrations that may not be conducting meaningful periodic
      surveys. We engage flag states with persistently high deficiency rates
      through targeted concentrated inspection campaigns and direct diplomatic
      communications from the secretariat. The commercial pressure available to
      us is increasingly useful: P&I clubs and informed charterers monitor PSC
      records closely, and the reputational cost of frequent detentions is
      beginning to register with vessel operators in ways that enforcement alone
      cannot achieve.</p>
  - question: >-
      Mauritania's accession expanded the MoU's reach. How are you thinking
      about the organisation's future geographic footprint?
    answer: >-
      <p>Mauritania's accession was significant for what it signals about the
      MoU's relevance beyond the Gulf of Guinea core, and we are in active
      dialogue with North African maritime administrations about coordination at
      the boundary between our mandate and the Mediterranean MoU's. My honest
      position on further geographic expansion, however, is that quality must
      precede coverage in our sequencing — I would rather consolidate the rigour
      of inspection and enforcement across our existing membership than extend
      the footprint faster than our secretariat capacity and training
      infrastructure can actually support. A credible regional PSC body with 22
      committed member states outweighs a nominal one with 30.</p>
  - question: >-
      How does the MoU's enforcement work connect to the broader maritime safety
      and environmental protection agenda in the region?
    answer: >-
      <p>PSC is the last enforcement line in a safety architecture that should
      also include robust flag state oversight, credible classification society
      surveys, and informed commercial pressure from charterers — and its power
      is amplified when it functions as part of a coherent system rather than as
      a substitute for those other mechanisms. We incorporated MARPOL compliance
      into our inspection protocols with significantly greater rigour over the
      last two reporting years, and we are seeing genuine improvement in
      pollution prevention equipment compliance as a result. This suggests that
      consistent, focused inspection does translate into fleet behaviour change
      when it is sustained across multiple reporting periods and multiple
      ports.</p>
  - question: >-
      What would you most want the international maritime community to
      understand about the Abuja MoU's work and its significance?
    answer: >-
      <p>That the work being done here is technically serious, not ceremonial —
      we inspect thousands of vessels each reporting cycle, have detained
      vessels that posed genuine threats to seafarer safety, and are building a
      regional fleet intelligence picture that did not previously exist. What we
      lack is the resource base to match the growing volume of vessel calls into
      our member state ports: inspection capacity has not kept pace with trade
      growth. The case for closing that gap through investment from member
      states, the international community, and the IMO's technical cooperation
      programmes is straightforward — safer shipping in African waters is not a
      regional interest, it is a global one.</p>
