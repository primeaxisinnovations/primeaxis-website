<p class="MsoNormal">Nigeria’s port system is at another
important turning point.</p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">The Federal Government has approved the
modernisation and upgrade of the Onne, Rivers, Delta and Calabar ports,
reopening a strategic question that has shaped the country’s maritime economy
for decades.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">Can Nigeria develop a more balanced network
of maritime gateways, or will Lagos continue to carry the overwhelming weight
of the country’s port economy?<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">The decision is significant because the
four ports are located within some of Nigeria’s important industrial, energy,
commercial and regional trade corridors.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">&nbsp;</span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">But approval is only the beginning.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">The larger question is whether modernising
these gateways can change how cargo moves, where businesses locate, how
shipping lines make port decisions and how efficiently Nigeria connects its
coastal infrastructure to the wider economy.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">That is where the real test begins.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">&nbsp;</span></p>

<h2><span lang="EN-US">THE GEOGRAPHY NIGERIA HAS NOT FULLY USED<o:p></o:p></span></h2>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">Nigeria does not lack maritime gateways.<br>
Its coastline provides access to several ports positioned to serve different
regions and economic activities.<br>
Lagos has developed into the country’s dominant commercial maritime centre,
supported by a concentration of terminals, shipping services, logistics
companies, manufacturers, warehouses, financial institutions and other
businesses.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">But Onne, Port Harcourt, Warri and Calabar
occupy strategic positions outside that concentration.<br>
Onne is closely connected to the Niger Delta’s industrial and energy economy.<br>
Port Harcourt serves a major commercial and industrial region. Warri sits
within an important industrial and energy corridor. Calabar provides a maritime
gateway to the eastern part of the country and has potential relevance to
regional trade.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">&nbsp;</span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">Their geography gives them an important
starting advantage, but location alone does not create a competitive port.<br>
A port becomes commercially important when vessels can call efficiently, cargo
can be handled reliably, inland connections function and businesses have a
reason to use the gateway.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">That is why the modernisation of the
eastern ports is ultimately an economic question, not simply an infrastructure
question.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<h2><span lang="EN-US">HOW LAGOS BECAME THE CENTRE<o:p></o:p></span></h2>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">The concentration of maritime activity
around Lagos did not happen by accident.<br>
Years of investment, terminal development, shipping activity, logistics
infrastructure and commercial concentration have reinforced the position of
Lagos within Nigeria’s port economy.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">The concessioning of port terminals from
the mid 2000s also changed the structure of port operations, introducing
private terminal operators and shifting greater attention towards investment,
efficiency and commercial performance.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">But the development of a dominant maritime
centre also creates a difficult question for a country as geographically large
as Nigeria.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">When maritime trade becomes heavily
concentrated in one commercial corridor, the efficiency of that corridor
becomes critical to the wider economy. The issue is therefore not whether Lagos
should remain important. It will.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">The issue is whether Nigeria can develop
other gateways strongly enough to provide credible commercial alternatives.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">The latest approval also comes against a
backdrop of concerns among industry stakeholders about the relative attention
given to Lagos in earlier modernisation plans, particularly as the initial
programme focused on Apapa and Tin Can Island.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">A stronger eastern port system would not
necessarily mean taking cargo away from Lagos. It could mean allowing cargo to
move through the gateway that makes the greatest commercial sense based on its
origin, destination, commodity and supply chain.<br>
That is the difference between having several ports and having a national port
system.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<h2><span lang="EN-US">WHAT HAS ACTUALLY BEEN APPROVED?<o:p></o:p></span></h2>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">The Federal Government has approved the
comprehensive modernisation and upgrade of Onne, Rivers, Delta and Calabar
ports.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">The Minister of Marine and Blue Economy,
Adegboyega Oyetola, said the expansion beyond Lagos is intended to complement
the earlier modernisation programme for Apapa and Tin Can Island and create
additional efficient gateways for international trade. The government says the
upgrades are expected to improve cargo handling, reduce vessel turnaround times
and strengthen regional connectivity.<br>
But the approval does not, by itself, establish the full investment structure
or implementation arrangements for the four ports.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">The public announcement has not provided a
comprehensive breakdown of the project costs, funding sources, engineering
specifications, procurement arrangements or completion dates for the eastern
port upgrades.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">These details matter because the scale and
complexity of port modernisation extend well beyond the rehabilitation of
terminal facilities.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">A credible programme must address the
condition of quay structures, navigational channels, cargo handling equipment,
access roads, digital systems and other infrastructure required for efficient
operations.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">&nbsp;</span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">It must also establish who will finance,
execute and maintain the different components.<br>
The financing question is therefore as important as the construction question.<br>
Will the investment be funded directly by government, private terminal
operators, concessionaires, public private partnerships, development finance or
a combination of different sources?<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">The answer will shape not only how quickly
the projects can be delivered, but also how responsibilities for performance
and maintenance are allocated.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">For port infrastructure, the investment
does not end when construction is completed.<br>
Channels must remain navigable.<br>
Equipment must be maintained and replaced.<br>
Access infrastructure must continue to function.<br>
Digital systems must evolve with the needs of trade.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">The distinction between approval and
implementation is therefore critical.<br>
The question is no longer simply whether the four ports will be modernised.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">It is how the investment will be
structured, who will deliver it, what each port is ultimately expected to
handle and how the infrastructure will be sustained once it is operational.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<h2><span lang="EN-US">THE BIGGER PORT SYSTEM<o:p></o:p></span></h2>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">The modernisation of Onne, Rivers, Delta
and Calabar is not being presented as a standalone intervention.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">It forms part of a wider expansion of
Nigeria’s maritime infrastructure, following the earlier approval for Apapa and
Tin Can Island in Lagos and alongside federal approvals for the development of
new deep seaports at Ibom, Bakassi, Agge, Gateway, Ondo and Bonny.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">&nbsp;</span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">That changes the scale of the conversation.<br>
Nigeria is not simply upgrading individual ports.<br>
It is attempting to expand the number of gateways through which the economy can
connect to international trade.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">&nbsp;</span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">The strategic challenge will be ensuring
that these investments complement rather than compete inefficiently with one
another.<br>
The question is therefore not simply how many ports Nigeria has.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">It is whether each gateway has a clear
commercial role, sufficient cargo demand and the road, rail, inland waterway
and logistics connections required to make that role viable.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">This aligns with the ministry’s stated
vision of an integrated maritime and logistics system in which ports, roads,
rail, inland waterways and other transport infrastructure work together to move
cargo.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">&nbsp;</span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">That is a much bigger proposition than port
rehabilitation.<br>
It is a question of how Nigeria designs its maritime economic architecture.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<h2><span lang="EN-US">THE PORT CANNOT BE MODERNISED IN ISOLATION<o:p></o:p></span></h2>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">A modern terminal cannot compensate for an
inefficient logistics chain.<br>
Roads matter.<br>
Rail matters.<br>
Customs processes matter.<br>
Warehousing matters.<br>
Digital systems matter.<br>
Security matters.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">&nbsp;</span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">The reliability of the entire cargo journey
matters. This is particularly important for the eastern ports.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">A vessel may arrive efficiently and cargo
may be discharged efficiently, but if trucks cannot move reliably, rail
connections are inadequate or cargo owners cannot predict delivery times, the
competitive advantage of the port is weakened.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">The port therefore has to be understood as
one part of a larger logistics system.<br>
The waterfront is only the beginning.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">The cargo must move from the terminal to
the factory, warehouse, distribution center, farm or final market. For export
cargo, the process must work in the opposite direction. That means the success
of the eastern ports will depend partly on infrastructure that sits beyond the
port boundary.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<h2><span lang="EN-US">THE CARGO QUESTION<o:p></o:p></span></h2>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">Modernisation cannot be separated from
commercial demand.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">A port can have new cranes, improved
channels and rehabilitated infrastructure, but those assets must ultimately be
supported by cargo. That raises an important question for each gateway.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">What cargo is the port intended to serve?<br>
Which industries are located within its natural hinterland?<br>
Which shipping lines could be attracted?<br>
Which manufacturers, exporters, importers and logistics operators would have a
commercial reason to use it?<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">These questions should shape investment
decisions before major capital is committed.<br>
Onne, Port Harcourt, Warri and Calabar do not need identical development
models.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">Their commercial roles should reflect their
locations, surrounding industries, cargo potential and connections to the wider
economy.<br>
A successful national port strategy should therefore be based not simply on
upgrading facilities, but on assigning clear economic roles to different
gateways.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<h2><span lang="EN-US">FROM PORT INFRASTRUCTURE TO INDUSTRIAL ECONOMY<o:p></o:p></span></h2>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">The strongest case for developing Nigeria’s
eastern ports may ultimately have little to do with the ports themselves.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">It is about what happens around them. A
competitive port can influence where factories locate.<br>
It can reduce the friction involved in importing industrial inputs.<br>
It can strengthen export channels.<br>
It can support distribution networks.<br>
It can improve regional trade.<br>
And it can make investment in surrounding industrial areas more attractive.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">This is where port policy becomes economic
policy.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">&nbsp;</span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">If Onne becomes more efficient, the benefit
should extend beyond terminal operators to the wider industrial and energy
economy.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">If Port Harcourt becomes more competitive,
manufacturers and businesses in its hinterland should gain stronger access to
maritime trade.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">If Warri is modernised effectively, the
Delta’s industrial economy should gain another reliable maritime gateway.<br>
If Calabar becomes commercially competitive, businesses in the eastern corridor
should have a stronger connection to international trade.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">&nbsp;</span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">The objective should therefore not simply
be more port activity. It should be more economic activity connected to the
ports.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<h2><span lang="EN-US">THE INTERNATIONAL LESSON<o:p></o:p></span></h2>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">The experience of successful port systems
elsewhere offers an important lesson.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">Tanger Med in Morocco is one example of how
a port can become part of a much wider economic strategy.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">Its development has been connected to
logistics and industrial activities, allowing the port to function as more than
a point where ships load and discharge cargo. Nigeria should not attempt to
copy Tanger Med.<br>
The countries, markets, geography and institutional environments are different.
The relevant lesson is more fundamental.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">Port infrastructure creates greater
economic value when it is connected deliberately to industry, logistics,
investment and trade. A port should not simply receive cargo.<br>
It should help organise economic activity around that cargo.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">For Nigeria, that means the eastern
gateways should be developed with the industries and trade corridors around
them firmly in view.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<h2><span lang="EN-US">THE IMPLEMENTATION TEST<o:p></o:p></span></h2>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">The approval is the beginning of the
process, not its conclusion.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">For the modernisation programme to produce
lasting value, each port needs a clear implementation framework.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">That framework should establish the
infrastructure required, the commercial role of the port, the financing
structure, procurement process, operational responsibilities, maintenance
arrangements and measurable performance targets.<br>
The sequencing will matter.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">Dredging without adequate terminal capacity
will not be enough.<br>
Terminal investment without reliable cargo evacuation will not be enough.<br>
Port infrastructure without cargo demand will not be enough. <br>
And cargo demand without predictable service will not be sustainable.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">The programme therefore needs to connect
infrastructure investment with actual trade flows.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">That requires engagement with shipping
lines, terminal operators, cargo owners, manufacturers, exporters, logistics
companies, financial institutions and the government agencies responsible for
roads, rail, Customs and port operations.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">The objective should be one coordinated
logistics system rather than a collection of separate infrastructure projects.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<h2><span lang="EN-US">THE COMMERCIAL TEST<o:p></o:p></span></h2>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">Ultimately, the market will determine
whether the eastern gateways become genuinely competitive.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">Cargo owners will ask whether using an
alternative port reduces cost and uncertainty. Shipping lines will consider
whether vessel calls are commercially viable. Manufacturers will consider
whether imported inputs can reach their facilities reliably. Exporters will
consider whether their products can reach international markets efficiently.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">Logistics operators will consider whether
road, rail and terminal connections work. Those decisions will determine
whether modernisation translates into sustained port activity. Government can
approve infrastructure.<br>
It can facilitate investment.<br>
It can establish policy.<br>
But long-term commercial relevance depends on whether businesses choose to use
the system.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">That is why the success of the programme
should ultimately be measured not only by what is constructed, but by what the
infrastructure enables.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">More efficient cargo movement.<br>
More reliable vessel calls.<br>
Stronger industrial connections.<br>
Greater regional trade.<br>
And a more predictable relationship between Nigerian businesses and
international markets.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<h2><span lang="EN-US">REBUILDING THE ECONOMICS OF THE PORT SYSTEM<o:p></o:p></span></h2>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">Nigeria’s maritime economy is entering a
period in which capacity alone will not be enough.<br>
The country has a large domestic market, significant industrial activity and a
strategic position within West Africa.<br>
The challenge is to connect those advantages to a port system capable of
serving the economy more efficiently. That requires a different approach to
port development.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">The eastern gateways do not need to become
copies of Lagos.<br>
They need to become commercially relevant gateways in their own right.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">Their development should be informed by the
cargo around them, the industries they can serve, the shipping services they
can attract and the inland connections required to move goods efficiently.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">That makes the opportunity larger than port
rehabilitation.<br>
It is an opportunity to change the geography of Nigeria’s maritime economy.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<h2><span lang="EN-US">PRIMEAXIS INSIGHT<o:p></o:p></span></h2>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">Nigeria does not simply need more ports. It
needs a port system that can move the economy.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">The modernisation of Onne, Rivers, Delta
and Calabar creates an opportunity to build greater balance into Nigeria’s
maritime infrastructure and connect more directly with economic activity beyond
Lagos. <br>
But infrastructure approval is not the final measure.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">The real measure will be whether the ports
become commercially useful enough for businesses and shipping lines to make
different choices.<br>
<br>
Whether ships call.<br>
Whether cargo moves efficiently.<br>
Whether factories gain better access to inputs.<br>
Whether exporters gain more reliable access to markets.<br>
Whether regional trade becomes easier.<br>
And whether the economic value generated by Nigeria’s maritime gateways reaches
beyond the waterfront.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">&nbsp;</span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">Lagos will remain central to Nigeria’s
maritime economy.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">The question is whether Nigeria can build a
system in which Lagos no longer carries the full weight of the country’s
maritime ambition. That is the real turning point.<br>
Not simply building more port infrastructure. But rebuilding the economics of
the port system itself.<o:p></o:p></span></p>
