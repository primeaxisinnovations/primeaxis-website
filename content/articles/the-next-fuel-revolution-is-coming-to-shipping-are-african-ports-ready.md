<p>The global shipping industry is moving towards a new generation of marine fuels. Liquefied natural gas, methanol, biofuels, ammonia and hydrogen are increasingly part of the conversation about how ships will operate as pressure grows to reduce greenhouse gas emissions.</p><p>But the transition is not simply about what fuel a vessel can use. It is also about where that fuel will be produced, stored, transported and supplied.</p><p>For Africa, this raises a strategic question. Can the continent position its ports to participate in the emerging marine fuel market, or will it remain largely an exporter of energy resources while importing the infrastructure, technology and services required by the shipping industry?</p><p>The answer will have implications for port competitiveness, energy investment, maritime trade and the continent's wider industrial ambitions.</p><h2>THE FLEET IS MOVING. ARE THE PORTS KEEPING UP?</h2><p>A report released by Lloyd's Register on 2 November 2026 highlights the scale of the infrastructure challenge.</p><p>According to the report, 1,942 alternative fuel capable vessels were on order in January 2026, representing 47 per cent of global orderbook tonnage. Yet the availability of bunkering infrastructure differs significantly across fuel types.</p><p>LNG bunkering was available at 222 ports worldwide, while only 25 ports were actively offering methanol bunkering. The first commercial ammonia bunkering operation was completed in Ulsan, South Korea, in April 2026.</p><p>The figures reveal a growing gap between vessel capability and the infrastructure required to support it.</p><p>The transition is unlikely to follow one uniform pathway. Biofuel blends are among the most immediately deployable options because they can largely use existing bunkering infrastructure.</p><p>&nbsp;Lloyd's Register recorded drop-in biofuel deliveries at 90 ports worldwide over the preceding 12 months.</p><p>Methanol is gaining commercial ground, while ammonia remains at an earlier stage of development. Hydrogen is still largely associated with short sea operations and demonstration projects.</p><p>For ports, this means investment decisions must be informed by credible demand, vessel deployment patterns, fuel availability and the routes ships are expected to serve.</p><h2>AFRICA HAS AN OPPORTUNITY. THE INFRASTRUCTURE QUESTION REMAINS.</h2><p>Africa's energy resources and strategic maritime position give the continent a potential role in the changing marine fuel supply chain.</p><p>However, producing energy is not the same as supplying the shipping industry. The commercial opportunity extends beyond fuel production to storage facilities, bunkering vessels, pipelines, port equipment, safety systems, certification, technical expertise and reliable distribution networks.</p><p>Without these supporting capabilities, the value generated by the transition may be captured elsewhere.</p><p>The concern is not whether African countries possess energy resources. It is whether they can develop the infrastructure and regulatory systems that connect those resources to maritime demand.</p><h2>THE READINESS GAP</h2><p>The scale of the challenge is reflected in assessments cited by the WestMED Initiative during its March 2026 report on the ECOWAS continental validation workshop on green ports.</p><p>The assessments indicated that nearly 75 per cent of African port authorities had yet to update their regulatory frameworks on alternative fuels.</p><p>&nbsp;Fewer than 20 per cent of ports were considered prepared for hydrogen or ammonia bunkering, while less than 15 per cent of personnel had received formal training on low or zero carbon fuels.</p><p>These figures, as reported by WestMED, point to a readiness gap that extends beyond physical infrastructure. Regulation, workforce competence, safety procedures and institutional coordination will all influence how quickly ports can participate in the emerging market.</p><p>A port cannot become an alternative fuel hub simply by announcing an ambition. It needs clear operating standards, emergency response capacity, trained personnel, investment plans and a dependable supply chain.</p><h2>POLICY IS MOVING. IMPLEMENTATION WILL MATTER.</h2><p>There are signs that the continental policy conversation is advancing.</p><p>In November 2025, the International Maritime Organization and the European Union launched the Future Ready Shipping in Africa project, backed by a €5 million European Union funding pledge.</p><p>The initiative includes technical assistance, feasibility studies and the development of bankable business cases for alternative fuel bunkering facilities and green shipping corridors in selected African ports. It also covers maritime governance, digitalisation and maritime education and training.</p><p>In April 2026, African Union ministers responsible for transport and energy endorsed key maritime frameworks, including Green Ports Guidelines, a continental strategy for reducing maritime greenhouse gas emissions, the Revised African Maritime Transport Charter and the Maritime Single Window.</p><p>The frameworks provide a policy direction for cleaner port operations, alternative fuels, digital transformation and stronger coordination across the continent.</p><p>But policy endorsement is only the starting point. The real test will be whether governments translate these commitments into funded programmes, enforceable standards, infrastructure projects and measurable improvements in port operations.</p><h2>LESSONS FROM EGYPT'S PORT READINESS APPROACH</h2><p>Egypt offers an instructive example of how port readiness can be approached.</p><p>In June 2026, the IMO's GreenVoyage2050 programme reported that experts had assessed five Egyptian ports: Ain Sokhna, Alexandria, Damietta, Dekheila and East Port Said.</p><p>The exercise examined existing facilities, expansion plans, fuel production and storage potential, bunkering opportunities, safety requirements and regulatory needs. Its findings are intended to inform Egypt's national action plan and alternative fuel roadmap.</p><p>The significance of this exercise lies in its practical approach. Rather than treating the transition as a general environmental ambition, it examines what individual ports need to become viable locations for future marine fuel supply.</p><p>African ports can draw an important lesson from this. Readiness must be assessed at port level, taking account of location, trade routes, energy availability, expected vessel demand and the cost of supporting infrastructure.</p><h2>THE COMPETITIVENESS TEST FOR AFRICAN PORTS</h2><p>The next phase of maritime competition will not be determined by cargo handling capacity alone.</p><p>&nbsp;The ability to provide reliable, safe and commercially viable services to vessels using new fuels could become an important factor in port selection and shipping route decisions.</p><p>For African ports, the investment question is therefore immediate. Which fuels are commercially realistic for their markets?&nbsp;</p><p>Which ports have the strongest potential to serve emerging demand? What partnerships can mobilise capital, technology and technical expertise? And how can local businesses and workers participate in the value chain?</p><p>There is also a need to avoid fragmented national approaches. Regional cooperation on standards, training, safety procedures and green shipping corridors could help reduce investment risks and strengthen Africa's position in international maritime trade.</p><p>The transition will require substantial capital, but it also presents an opportunity to attract new investment into port infrastructure, energy systems, maritime services and skills development.</p><h2>PRIMEAXIS INSIGHT</h2><p><span style="font-weight: normal;">Africa's maritime future will be shaped not only by the number of ships calling at its ports, but also by the services, infrastructure and expertise those ports can provide.</span></p><p>The alternative marine fuel transition presents an opportunity to move beyond the traditional role of exporting raw energy resources. But that opportunity will not materialise automatically.</p><p>Countries that connect energy production to port infrastructure, bunkering services, safety regulation, technical skills and investment will be better positioned to capture value from the changing shipping industry.</p><p>The strategic question for African policymakers and port authorities is no longer whether the global shipping industry is changing. It is whether Africa is preparing to participate in the business opportunities that change will create.</p><p>For Africa, the next fuel revolution must become more than an energy transition. It must become a maritime competitiveness strategy.</p><h2>REFERENCES</h2><p>1.
