<h2>From Policy to Performance</h2><p>For decades, African maritime policy has focused on one objective:&nbsp;</p><p>Increasing indigenous participation in shipping.</p><p>Cabotage laws were introduced.</p><p>Local content policies were enacted.</p><p>Maritime administrations strengthened regulatory frameworks.</p><p>These were important first steps.</p><p>But policy alone does not create a competitive shipping industry.</p><p>The real measure of success is not how many policies are enacted.</p><p>It is how much value Africa captures from the movement of its own trade.</p><p>Because indigenous shipping is not simply about participation.</p><p>It is about ownership.</p><p>Capability.</p><p>And value capture.</p><h3>1. Policy Was the Beginning—Not the Destination</h3><p>Cabotage policies were designed to give indigenous operators greater access to domestic shipping markets.</p><p>Across Africa, they have created new opportunities for local participation.</p><p>Yet many countries continue to face the same challenge.</p><p>Domestic operators often lack access to modern vessels, affordable financing, shipbuilding capacity, and repair infrastructure.</p><p>As a result, foreign operators continue to dominate significant segments of commercial shipping, even where protective policies exist.</p><p>The gap is no longer policy.</p><p>It is capability.</p><h3>2. The Hidden Cost of Dependence</h3><p>Every year, African economies lose billions of dollars through freight payments, vessel chartering, overseas repairs, and imported maritime services.</p><p>Much of the value created by African trade is therefore captured outside the continent.</p><p>The ships transport African cargo.</p><p>The ports handle African trade.</p><p>But a significant share of the commercial returns flows elsewhere.</p><p>This is not simply a shipping issue.</p><p>It is an industrial and economic development challenge.</p><h3>3. Indigenous Shipping Requires More Than Indigenous Shipowners</h3><p>True maritime capability extends beyond owning vessels.</p><p>It requires an integrated ecosystem that includes:</p><p><ul><li>Competitive indigenous shipping companies</li><li>Modern shipbuilding and repair facilities</li><li>Sustainable maritime financing</li><li>Marine insurance and ship finance</li><li>Skilled maritime professionals</li><li>Strong regulatory institutions</li><li>Efficient ports and logistics corridors</li></ul></p><p>Without these foundations, indigenous shipping remains dependent on external capabilities.</p><h3>4. Financing Remains the Missing Catalyst</h3><p>Across much of Africa, access to long-term, affordable capital remains one of the greatest barriers to indigenous shipping growth.</p><p>Ships are capital-intensive assets.</p><p>Without effective financing mechanisms, many local operators struggle to modernise their fleets or compete internationally.</p><p>Closing this financing gap is essential if indigenous operators are to move beyond survival and achieve long-term competitiveness.</p><h3>5. Ownership Is Only Valuable If It Creates Domestic Value</h3><p>Owning more vessels is important.</p><p>But ownership alone is not enough.</p><p>The real objective is to retain more value within African economies.</p><p>That means:</p><p><ul><li>Building and repairing ships locally where commercially viable</li><li>Developing African marine engineering capabilities</li><li>Expanding maritime manufacturing and supply chains</li><li>Increasing local employment</li><li>Strengthening supporting industries such as insurance, finance, classification, and technology</li></ul></p><p>This is how shipping becomes an engine of industrial development rather than simply a transport service.</p><h3>6. From Participation to Value Capture</h3><p>The next phase of Africa's maritime development is not simply about increasing participation.</p><p>It is about increasing ownership.</p><p>Not only ownership of ships—</p><p>But ownership of maritime capability.</p><p>Ownership of industrial capacity.</p><p>Ownership of technology.</p><p>Ownership of knowledge.</p><p>Ownership of value.</p><p>Because countries that own more of the maritime value chain retain more of the economic benefits it creates.</p><h3>FINAL THOUGHT</h3><p>Cabotage was never meant to be the finish line.</p><p>It was meant to be the foundation.</p><p>The real test of indigenous shipping success is not how many local operators enter the market.</p><p>It is whether Africa builds the capability to own more ships, finance more ships, build more ships, repair more ships, employ more maritime professionals, and retain more value from its own trade.</p><p>Because the future of African shipping will not be defined by policy alone.</p><p>It will be defined by how much of the maritime value chain Africa ultimately owns.</p>
