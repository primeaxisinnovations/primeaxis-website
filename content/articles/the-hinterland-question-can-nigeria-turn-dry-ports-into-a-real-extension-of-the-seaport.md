<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-GB">Nigeria is handling more cargo.<br>
The harder question is whether that cargo is moving efficiently enough from the
waterfront into the wider economy.<br>
The numbers make the question increasingly important.<br>
<br>
Nigeria’s seaports handled 71.21 million metric tonnes of cargo in 2023. In
2024, throughput rose 45.1 percent to 103.34 million tonnes. In 2025, it
increased another 24.8 percent to more than 129.3 million tonnes.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-GB">The growth has continued into 2026.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-GB">In the second quarter alone, Nigerian
seaports handled 35.7 million metric tonnes, 12.3 percent more than the 31.8
million tonnes recorded in the same period of 2025. Container traffic reached
602,392 TEUs, while ocean-going vessel calls increased by 14.4 percent to
1,201.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-GB">Nigeria’s maritime gateway is handling more
cargo, more containers and more vessel activity.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-GB">But a port is not an economy.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-GB">&nbsp;</span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-GB">Cargo creates broader economic value only
when it can move efficiently to the places where it will be consumed,
processed, manufactured, stored, distributed or exported.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-GB">That is where Nigeria’s hinterland question
begins.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<h2><span lang="EN-GB">THE PORT CANNOT END AT THE WATERFRONT<o:p></o:p></span></h2>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-GB">The original promise of the Inland Dry Port
was straightforward.<br>
Extend the functions of the seaport inland.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-GB">Move cargo closer to businesses outside the
coastal corridor. Connect inland locations to maritime trade through road and
rail. Take pressure away from the seaports. Bring port services closer to cargo
owners.<br>
In other words, the dry port was never supposed to be simply another facility
where containers are parked.<br>
<!--[if !supportLineBreakNewLine]--><br>
<!--[endif]--><o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-GB">It was supposed to extend the reach of the
seaport.<br>
Nigeria has spent years developing that concept through facilities including
Kaduna, Dala, Funtua, Heipang, Erunmu, Isiala Ngwa and Jauri, at different
stages of development and operation. But the strategic question has changed. It
is no longer simply whether Nigeria has dry ports.<br>
It is whether those facilities function as parts of one national cargo system. There
is a fundamental difference between building multiple facilities and building a
connected logistics network. A dry port on its own is infrastructure.<br>
A dry port connected reliably to a seaport, railway, road network, Customs
process, warehouse, manufacturer and market is logistics architecture.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-GB">The economic value lies in the connection.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<h2><span lang="EN-GB">THE REGULATORY RESET<o:p></o:p></span></h2>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-GB">That makes the current institutional
transition particularly significant.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-GB">Following the enactment of the Nigerian
Ports Economic Regulatory Agency Act, 2026, the Federal Government directed the
transfer of Inland Dry Port functions from the Nigerian Shippers’ Council, now
NPERA, to the Nigerian Ports Authority.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-GB">The stated objective is to create a clearer
separation between port economic regulation, development and operations, while
placing Inland Dry Port development within the wider port infrastructure and
operational network. The transition is now moving from policy to
implementation.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-GB">On September 21, NPA and NPERA began
technical discussions on the transfer, including the definition of
institutional responsibilities and the prevention of duplication. Director
General and CEO of NPERA, Dr Pius Akutah has also proposed stronger inter-agency
coordination involving relevant institutions across the maritime and transport
chain. That institutional alignment matters. But the handover itself is not the
outcome. The outcome is what happens to cargo.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-GB">Does the new structure make movement from
the seaport into the hinterland faster?<br>
Does it reduce unnecessary interfaces?<br>
Does it improve predictability?<br>
Does it make inland terminals easier for cargo owners to use?<br>
Does it make rail, road and inland terminals work together more effectively?<br>
And can the system operate as one logistics chain rather than a collection of
agencies, terminals and transport modes?<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-GB">Those are the measures that will determine
whether the reform creates economic value.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-GB">&nbsp;</span></p>

<h2><span lang="EN-GB">RAIL IS PART OF THE DRY PORT QUESTION<o:p></o:p></span></h2>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-GB">The performance of an inland dry port
cannot be separated from the transportation network connecting it to the
seaport. This is one of the clearest tests of whether Nigeria is building a
system or simply developing facilities.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-GB">The Nigerian Shippers’ Council’s assessment
of the Kaduna Inland Dry Port identified the absence of rail transportation as
a significant constraint on its overall performance. Its reporting has also
identified rail connectivity as a constraint for other inland dry port
operations. That tells a larger story. The success of an inland terminal is
partly determined outside its gates. If cargo arrives at the seaport but cannot
move reliably by rail or road, the existence of an inland facility does not by
itself solve the logistics problem. The same principle applies across the
chain.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-GB">The vessel, terminal, railway, truck, dry
port, Customs process, warehouse and final market may belong to different
institutions, but economically they represent one journey.<br>
When that journey is fragmented, the cost is ultimately carried through time,
uncertainty, additional handling and logistics inefficiency. This is why cargo
evacuation should not be treated as a narrow port operation. It is a national
trade infrastructure question.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<h2><span lang="EN-GB">THE CARGO IS ALREADY HERE<o:p></o:p></span></h2>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-GB">Nigeria does not have to manufacture the
demand for a stronger inland logistics system. The cargo growth is already
visible. More than 129 million tonnes moved through the ports in 2025, while
2026 is continuing to show strong activity.<br>
The challenge is to make the infrastructure behind those volumes capable of
supporting a larger trading economy. That means thinking beyond evacuation. Cargo
movement is not simply about getting containers away from the quay. It is about
connecting imports to factories and distribution networks. <br>
It is about connecting agricultural and industrial production to export
gateways.<br>
It is about giving businesses outside the coastal corridor a more direct
relationship with international trade.<br>
It is about making the Nigerian port system capable of serving not just Lagos,
but the economic geography of Nigeria.<br>
And eventually, the wider West African market. That is the strategic promise of
the hinterland.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<h2><span lang="EN-GB">FROM PORT CAPACITY TO ECONOMIC VALUE<o:p></o:p></span></h2>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-GB">Nigeria’s port story is changing. For
years, the central conversation was about whether the country could attract
vessels, modernise terminals and increase cargo throughput. Those questions
remain important. But rising cargo volumes create another test. <o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-GB">Can Nigeria turn maritime activity into
wider economic activity? That requires the seaport to be understood not as an
isolated coastal facility, but as the first node in a national logistics
network. The vessel should connect to the terminal. The terminal should connect
to rail and road. The inland dry port should connect to the shipper. The
shipper should connect to the factory, warehouse, farm or distribution center. And
the entire chain should ultimately connect Nigerian production and consumption
to international markets. That is the difference between infrastructure and
infrastructure that changes an economy. <o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-GB">Nigeria has spent years developing the
physical idea of the Inland Dry Port. The next challenge is to make the system
around it work. Because the real measure of a dry port will not be how many
containers it can hold. It will be how much economic activity can move because
it exists.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<h2><span lang="EN-GB">THE HINTERLAND IS THE NEXT TEST<o:p></o:p></span></h2>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-GB">Nigeria’s maritime story is entering a
different phase. The question is no longer simply whether the country can
handle more cargo. It is whether the country can move the value of that cargo
deeper into the economy. That requires a different definition of port
competitiveness. The port of the future cannot stop at the waterfront. It must
reach the factory, the warehouse, the farm, the exporter, the distribution
centre, the regional market, and ultimately, the African consumer.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-GB">That means the future of Nigeria’s port
system will not be determined by the waterfront alone.<br>
It will be determined by what happens after the vessel docks.<br>
Can cargo move inland efficiently?<br>
Can businesses outside the coastal corridor connect predictably to
international trade?<br>
Can Nigerian producers reach export markets with greater reliability?<br>
Can the seaport, railway, road network and inland terminals function as one
system?<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-GB">These are bigger questions than the number
of containers a terminal can handle.<br>
They are questions about whether maritime infrastructure can translate into
broader economic capacity.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<h2><span lang="EN-GB">PRIMEAXIS INSIGHT<o:p></o:p></span></h2>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-GB">Nigeria does not simply need more ports.<br>
It needs a port system that can move the economy.<br>
The strategic question is no longer whether cargo can enter Nigeria.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-GB">It is whether Nigeria can move that cargo
efficiently enough to connect the waterfront to the factory, the warehouse, the
farm, the exporter, the regional market and ultimately the wider African
economy.<br>
That is where the value of port infrastructure will be decided. Not at the quay
alone, but across the entire journey from ship to market.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-GB">The future competitiveness of Nigeria’s
ports may therefore depend less on where cargo enters the country and more on
how far, how efficiently and how predictably that cargo can move once it
arrives.<br>
The port of the future cannot stop at the waterfront. It must reach the
economy.<o:p></o:p></span></p>
