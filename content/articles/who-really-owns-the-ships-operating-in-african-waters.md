<p>A ship's flag identifies the country responsible for regulating it. Its registration documents establish its legal identity, while its ownership records should provide a basis for determining who is accountable for its operations.</p><p><br></p><p>But these records do not always reveal the full picture.</p><p><br></p><p>Behind a registered company may be several layers of corporate ownership, nominee arrangements or private interests that make it difficult to establish who ultimately controls a vessel. In other cases, ships operate under flags they are not authorised to fly, using fraudulent registration documents or the identity of another vessel.</p><p><br></p><p>This is no longer a peripheral concern for international shipping. It has become an issue of maritime security, regulatory credibility and the integrity of global trade.</p><p><br></p><p>In April 2026, the International Maritime Organization (IMO) Legal Committee approved new guidelines to improve transparency and due diligence in ship registration and prevent fraudulent registrations and misuse of flags. The decision followed reports that 529 ships had falsely flown the flag of a country in the preceding year, with nearly 40 IMO member states affected.</p><p>The scale of the problem raises a question African maritime administrations can no longer afford to overlook: how effectively do they establish who owns, controls and takes responsibility for ships operating under their flags?</p><h2>THE PROBLEM BEHIND THE FLAG</h2><p>Ship registration is more than an administrative process. Under the United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea (UNCLOS), a vessel's nationality connects it to a flag state, which is expected to exercise jurisdiction and control over it.</p><p><br></p><p>Article 91 addresses the nationality of ships and the conditions for granting the right to fly a flag. Article 94 places responsibility on flag states to exercise effective jurisdiction and control over vessels registered under their flags.</p><p><br></p><p>The distinction between a registered owner and a beneficial owner is important. A registered owner is the individual or company formally recorded as owning a vessel. The beneficial owner is the person or interest that ultimately enjoys the benefits of ownership or exercises effective control, even where that interest is held through other companies or arrangements.</p><p><br></p><p>Where ownership structures are opaque, regulators, financial institutions, insurers and enforcement agencies may struggle to establish who is ultimately responsible for a vessel.</p><p><br></p><p>Fraudulent registration creates another layer of risk. The IMO identifies practices including the continued use of a flag after registration has expired or been terminated, fraudulent representations to the organisation, falsified registration documents and manipulation of Automatic Identification System (AIS) information.</p><p><br></p><p>These practices can conceal a vessel's identity, complicate enforcement and create opportunities for illicit activity. They can also expose legitimate shipping interests to increased scrutiny when a country's flag becomes associated with vessels involved in sanctions evasion or other unlawful operations.</p><h2>AFRICAN FLAGS UNDER INCREASING SCRUTINY</h2><p>The issue has particular relevance for Africa.</p><p>A March 2026 analysis by the Institute for Security Studies, titled African Ship Registries: A Safe Harbour for Shadow Fleets?, examined the growing use of African flags by high risk vessels and the implications for maritime governance.</p><p><br></p><p>The analysis identified significant increases in registered tonnage in several African registries, including Benin, The Gambia, Comoros, Guinea and Sierra Leone. It also highlighted concerns about vessels suspected of links to shadow fleet networks and cases involving the fraudulent use of national flags.</p><p><br></p><p>One example was the tanker Range Vale, which Germany identified as part of Russia's shadow fleet after it falsely claimed registration in Zimbabwe. Zimbabwe subsequently notified the IMO that a fraudulent entity had issued the registration documents. The vessel later operated under Sierra Leone's flag.</p><p><br></p><p>These developments should not be interpreted as evidence that African ship registries are inherently illegitimate. Nor should legitimate African flag states be treated as interchangeable with unauthorised registration operators.</p><p><br></p><p>The concern is whether national administrations have the systems, resources and oversight mechanisms to verify applications, authenticate documents, monitor vessels and supervise any private entities involved in registration.</p><p><br></p><p>A flag is an expression of national authority. When it is misused, the consequences extend beyond the vessel concerned. A country can face reputational damage, increased port inspection scrutiny, insurance complications and questions about the credibility of its maritime administration.</p><p><br></p><p>For African countries seeking to attract shipping investment and strengthen their position in global trade, the integrity of their registries is an economic concern as much as a regulatory one.</p><h2>NIGERIA'S OWNERSHIP AND REGISTRATION QUESTION</h2><p>Nigeria has an established legal and administrative framework for ship registration.</p><p><br></p><p>The Merchant Shipping Act 2007 sets out the conditions for ownership and registration of Nigerian ships. The Nigerian Maritime Administration and Safety Agency (NIMASA) Act 2007 provides for the agency's maritime administration responsibilities, while the Coastal and Inland Shipping (Cabotage) Act 2003 establishes additional registration requirements for vessels engaged in domestic coastal trade.</p><p><br></p><p>NIMASA's Nigerian Ship Registration Office is responsible for registering vessels under these laws. The agency's published registration information identifies several categories of registration, including merchant ships, fishing vessels, ships under construction, bareboat&nbsp;</p><p>chartered vessels and vessels registered under the Cabotage regime.</p><p><br></p><p>The legal framework also establishes ownership conditions. Under the Merchant Shipping Act, registration under the Nigerian flag is generally limited to vessels owned by Nigerian citizens, qualifying Nigerian companies and partnerships, and other persons prescribed under the law.</p><p><br></p><p>Certain foreign owned vessels may also qualify under the provisions for bareboat charter registration.</p><p>These are important legal and administrative requirements. However, establishing the identity of a company applying to register a vessel is not necessarily the same as establishing the natural person or persons who ultimately own or control that company.</p><p><br></p><p>This distinction deserves attention, particularly where ownership involves multiple corporate entities, joint ventures or other arrangements.</p><p><br></p><p>There is also the question of access to information. The 2026 Nigeria Shipping Practice Guide published by Chambers and Partners indicates that ship ownership and mortgage registry information is not publicly accessible. A person who is not the vessel's owner must make a formal application to the Nigerian Ship Registration Office to conduct a search on its registration status or any mortgage over it.</p><p>Restricted access does not, by itself, establish that a registry is deficient. Some information may legitimately require protection. But the balance between confidentiality and transparency matters. Shipowners, charterers, financiers, insurers and other legitimate commercial interests need reliable ways to conduct due diligence.</p><p><br></p><p>For Nigeria, the challenge is therefore twofold: maintaining a credible registration system and ensuring that the information necessary to establish accountability can be verified by appropriate parties.</p><h2>OWNERSHIP MUST ALSO MAKE ECONOMIC SENSE</h2><p>The ownership question also connects to Nigeria's wider ambition to develop indigenous shipping capacity.</p><p><br></p><p>Having more vessels registered under the Nigerian flag does not automatically translate into a stronger national shipping industry. The economic value of vessel ownership depends on whether operators can secure cargo, obtain financing, maintain vessels, meet regulatory obligations and compete sustainably.</p><p><br></p><p>In PrimeAxis Executive Conversations, Aminu Umar, President of the Nigerian Chamber of Shipping, addressed the importance of access to capital in developing indigenous vessel ownership. Oluseyi Oluyede, General Manager of Maritime Shipping &amp; Ocean International Resources Ltd, discussed the commercial and operational realities of building shipping capacity, including the need to secure cargo and develop the competence to operate vessels effectively.</p><p><br></p><p>Their contributions point to an important distinction: registration is a legal starting point, not proof of commercial strength.</p><p><br></p><p>For Nigeria, a credible ship registry should support a shipping industry capable of attracting investment, meeting international standards and retaining more value within the domestic economy. That requires confidence not only in the vessels being registered but also in the ownership structures and operators behind them.</p><h2>WHAT MUST AFRICAN MARITIME ADMINISTRATIONS ADDRESS?</h2><p>The IMO's April 2026 guidelines provide a timely framework for strengthening ship registration procedures. They address governance and control over registration activities, quality assurance, due diligence on ownership and ship identity, eligibility checks, and information sharing.</p><p><br></p><p>The guidelines are intended to assist new and existing flag state registries in improving verification and oversight. They are particularly significant because there is currently no binding international framework specifically regulating ship registration.</p><p><br></p><p>African maritime administrations need to examine whether their existing systems can establish the identity of applicants, verify ownership structures, authenticate registration documents and maintain accurate records throughout a vessel's registration period.</p><p><br></p><p>Where private operators or third-party service providers are involved, governments must retain effective oversight. Responsibility for a national flag cannot be outsourced without accountability.</p><p><br></p><p>There is also a need for stronger cooperation between flag states, port states, maritime administrations, financial institutions and relevant security agencies. Information sharing can help identify suspicious vessels and registration claims before they become larger enforcement or reputational problems.</p><p><br></p><p>For Nigeria, NIMASA has an opportunity to demonstrate how its registration framework addresses these emerging risks and how it intends to align its procedures with the new IMO guidance.</p><h2>PRIMEAXIS INSIGHT</h2><p>Africa's maritime ambitions will depend not only on the number of ships it registers, the ports it develops or the cargo it handles, but also on the credibility of the institutions governing its maritime assets.</p><p><br></p><p>A ship's flag should represent a verifiable relationship between a vessel and the state responsible for it. Its ownership should be traceable to the extent necessary to establish accountability. Its registration should withstand scrutiny.</p><p><br></p><p>For Nigeria, strengthening the credibility of the ship registry is also part of building investor confidence in indigenous shipping. The country needs more than vessels carrying its flag. It needs a system in which ownership, control and regulatory responsibility can be established with confidence.</p><p>Ultimately, the question is not simply who owns the ship on paper. It is who controls it, who benefits from it and who can be held accountable when something goes wrong.</p>
