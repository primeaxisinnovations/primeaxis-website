<p>When cargo disappears, imported vehicles arrive with missing components or containers remain trapped in terminals while charges accumulate, the financial consequences rarely stop with the immediate dispute. Importers absorb unexpected costs, exporters risk losing overseas buyers and businesses face delays that undermine commercial relationships. Ultimately, these failures affect the cost and reliability of doing business through Nigerian ports.</p><p style="margin: 0px 0px 12px;">For years, complaints about cargo losses, disputed shipping charges, demurrage and poor service delivery have exposed the difficult relationship between port users and the companies responsible for moving, handling and storing their goods. The central question has been whether customers can obtain redress when service providers fail to meet their obligations.</p><p>The emergence of the Nigerian Port Economic Regulatory Agency (NPERA), following the transition from the Nigerian Shippers' Council, has raised expectations of a stronger regulatory response. But a new institutional identity and expanded statutory mandate will mean little if commercial operators continue to treat customer complaints as routine disputes that can be delayed or negotiated away.</p><p>Recent cases handled by NPERA offer an early indication of how the agency intends to assert its authority. They also reveal the scale of financial exposure facing port users and the difficulties involved in securing compensation when cargo is lost, damaged or delayed.</p><p>One of the most consequential cases involved COSCO Shipping Lines Nigeria Limited and MOI Foods Nigeria Limited. The dispute concerned the shortage of 1,099 bags of raw cashew nuts and cocoa beans from seven export containers transported from the exporter's warehouse to Apapa Port in Lagos. The value of the missing cargo was put at approximately ₦345 million.</p><p>MOI Foods maintained that it had paid COSCO for freight forwarding, documentation and inland transportation under a comprehensive service arrangement. It argued that it had not independently appointed the third party haulage operators and that responsibility for their appointment and supervision rested with the shipping company.</p><p>Evidence presented to NPERA included warehouse weighbridge records, cargo tallies and independent inspection reports. A particularly significant finding involved one container in which a shortage of 15.573 metric tonnes of cocoa was detected before the container cleared Customs and departed Nigerian waters. The discovery raised serious questions about cargo integrity during inland transit, even where container seals remained intact.</p><p>Following a conciliation process, NPERA held COSCO commercially responsible for the shortage and directed the carrier to begin immediate settlement negotiations with MOI Foods. It also required COSCO to submit a progress report by November 30, 2026, while recommending stronger cargo tracking, more rigorous vetting of haulage operators and closer supervision during transit.</p><p>The determination has implications beyond the value of the missing goods. It challenges the assumption that a shipping company can distance itself from the conduct of subcontracted logistics providers when those providers operate within a service arrangement managed by the carrier. For exporters, the case raises a fundamental question about who bears responsibility when cargo entrusted to a logistics chain does not arrive intact.</p><p>However, the distinction between a regulatory determination and completed compensation is important. NPERA has established COSCO's commercial responsibility and directed settlement negotiations. The agency's published account does not confirm that MOI Foods has received the ₦345 million or an agreed compensation amount.</p><p>Another intervention involved Grimaldi Nigeria Limited and importer Owen Eburajolo, whose 2021 Mercedes Benz GLE 53 allegedly arrived with its original equipment manufacturer steering wheel components replaced by inferior aftermarket parts.</p><p>The importer submitted a replacement invoice of US$8,396.22, covering original equipment components, electronic coding and calibration. Grimaldi's settlement offers reportedly increased from US$750 to US$3,800 but were rejected as inadequate.</p><p>The dispute exposed a familiar weakness in maritime claims management: the separation between local shipping agents and overseas principals or protection and indemnity insurers responsible for approving compensation. Grimaldi's local representative explained that it did not have direct authority over those decisions.</p><p>Following its review of the carriage documents and the parties' submissions, NPERA directed Grimaldi Nigeria to pay US$6,000 to the importer as full and final settlement by November 25, 2026. The agency's November 21 announcement described the case as resolved and used the language of recovery in its headline. However, its detailed account records a payment directive and deadline, not independent confirmation that the money had been received. The distinction matters because regulatory intervention must ultimately be judged by delivery of the remedy.</p><p>The agency has also reported a significant outcome in a dispute involving NIPCO Gas Limited and SIFAX Off-Dock Terminal. Ten containers became the subject of a disagreement over detention and storage charges exceeding ₦117 million after a Customs regulatory intervention disrupted their release.</p><p>The containers had initially been approved for release under a Customs Bank Bond on September 24. Haulage operations commenced on October 3 but were halted following a subsequent Customs directive for further document verification. Customs eventually cleared the consignment for release on October 23.</p><p>SIFAX reportedly maintained that additional demurrage and truck detention charges were payable despite the regulatory hold. NPERA challenged the imposition of substantial charges arising from a delay caused by regulatory intervention. Its Complaints Unit conducted an on-site inspection and required the terminal to escalate the disputed invoice of ₦117.16 million to its corporate headquarters for a full commercial waiver.</p><p>In its November 6 announcement, NPERA described the dispute as resolved and said its intervention had saved NIPCO Gas more than ₦100 million in detention and storage charges. The agency also directed a review of the terminal's billing structure against approved baseline tariffs and indicated that its tariff division would examine the terminal's tariff schedule and approval status.</p><p>The case raises an important regulatory issue: port users should not automatically bear the financial consequences of delays arising from government directives, particularly where they have secured the necessary release approvals.</p><p>Beyond individual claims, NPERA's engagement with Maersk Line Nigeria in October addressed a wider problem affecting Nigerian exporters. The agency challenged a disputed US$3,000 per container surcharge associated with disruptions to shipping networks arising from the Middle East crisis. It also raised concerns about export congestion, delayed cargo evacuation and waiver commitments that customers said had not been honoured.</p><p>Maersk attributed the disruption to geopolitical developments and vessel congestion at transshipment hubs, including Abidjan and Tema. The carrier outlined operational changes, including the use of dedicated feeder vessels, and agreed to escalate requests for full or partial surcharge relief to its global headquarters on a case by case basis.</p><p>NPERA challenged the surcharge, arguing that imposing additional costs on shipments already affected by disrupted voyages placed an unfair burden on Nigerian exporters. The agency also warned that the charges could undermine export competitiveness and encourage businesses to consider alternative regional trade corridors.</p><p>The matter, however, remained subject to a decision by Maersk's global management. The company's local representatives said they would present affected exporters' cases for consideration. NPERA's intervention therefore placed the surcharge under regulatory scrutiny, but its published account did not establish that the disputed charge had been cancelled or refunded.</p><p>These cases are unfolding as NPERA begins implementing its enabling law and establishing the institutional structures required to exercise its mandate. On November 24, Director General Dr Pius Akutah identified excessive port charges, poor service delivery, operational inefficiencies, stakeholder confidence and financial sustainability as key priorities.</p><p>He also disclosed plans to engage the National Assembly on implementation of the new regulatory framework and its expected economic benefits, including lower import costs, reduced charges and improved competitiveness.</p><p>On November 25, Akutah further outlined a strategic blueprint for a financially sustainable, technologically driven and competitive regulator. He stressed that the transition from the Shippers' Council to NPERA must represent more than a change of name. It requires institutional capacity, clear structures and the ability to deliver effective regulation.</p><p>The priorities are interconnected. Nigeria cannot build a competitive port system if cargo owners remain exposed to unexplained losses, unpredictable charges and weak dispute resolution. Nor can the regulator deliver lasting change without the technical capacity, financial independence and institutional credibility required to enforce its decisions.</p><p>For businesses, the consequences are immediate. Shipping costs and cargo integrity influence the landed cost of imports, the competitiveness of exports and the confidence of companies making long term trade commitments. Where port users cannot secure redress, the additional costs eventually travel through the supply chain and reach businesses and consumers.</p><p>NPERA therefore faces a test that cannot be settled by the number of complaints received or stakeholder meetings convened. The agency must demonstrate that its decisions lead to compliance, that approved tariffs are respected and that port users can obtain remedies within reasonable timelines.</p><h2>PRIMEAXIS INSIGHT</h2><p>NPERA's early interventions suggest that the regulatory balance in Nigeria's ports may be shifting. Its handling of the COSCO cargo shortage, the Grimaldi vehicle component dispute and the NIPCO Gas demurrage case shows that commercial practices are attracting closer scrutiny. The cases also demonstrate that port users have a regulatory channel through which they can challenge losses and charges.</p><p>But the real test is whether these interventions become consistent regulatory practice rather than isolated successes. A finding of commercial responsibility is not the same as compensation received. A waiver request is not the same as a confirmed refund. A challenge to a surcharge is not the same as its cancellation.</p><p>The agency must therefore make the outcomes of its interventions visible. Port users and the wider industry need to know whether compensation was paid, disputed charges were reversed, tariff violations were corrected and operators complied with regulatory decisions.</p><p>Nigeria's new port regulator has an opportunity to change the terms of accountability across the maritime logistics chain. Its credibility will depend on whether cargo owners recover their losses, unjustified charges are reversed and service providers change the practices that expose Nigerian businesses to avoidable costs.</p><p>When cargo goes missing, the question is no longer simply who is responsible. It is whether the regulator can ensure that responsibility has consequences.</p>
