<h2>The Cost of Inefficiency</h2><p>Africa has rightly recognized that efficient ports are essential to unlocking trade, attracting investment, and realizing the promise of the African Continental Free Trade Area (AfCFTA).</p><p>Across the continent, governments are investing heavily in deep-sea ports, container terminals, logistics parks, and maritime corridors. More than 90% of Africa's international trade moves by sea, making ports the backbone of the continent's economy.</p><p>Yet while infrastructure is expanding rapidly, logistics costs remain stubbornly high.</p><p>The paradox is difficult to ignore:</p><p>Africa is building world-class ports, yet many businesses still face some of the world's highest costs of moving goods.</p><p>The reason is straightforward.</p><p>Ports are only one part of the logistics chain.</p><p>If everything beyond the quay remains inefficient, expanding the port simply shifts congestion further inland.</p><p><br></p><p>1. <b>Africa's Ports Are Growing for Good Reason</b></p><p>The expansion is both necessary and strategic.</p><p>Cargo volumes have grown significantly over the past decade, driven by population growth, urbanization, industrialization, and rising consumer demand.</p><p>At the same time, global shipping has shifted toward much larger vessels that require deeper drafts, larger terminals, and more advanced cargo-handling equipment.</p><p>AfCFTA is expected to accelerate intra-African trade further, placing even greater pressure on existing port infrastructure.</p><p>Strategically located ports such as Tangier Med, Port Said, Lekki, Tema, Mombasa, Durban, and Dar es Salaam are positioning themselves as regional gateways and transshipment hubs.</p><p>Infrastructure investment is therefore essential.</p><p>But infrastructure alone is not enough.</p><p><br></p><p>2.<b> The Real Cost Problem Begins After the Ship Berths</b></p><p>For many importers and exporters, the highest costs do not occur at sea.</p><p>They begin once the cargo arrives.</p><p>According to the African Development Bank (AfDB), port processing and transport costs in Africa remain roughly 50% higher than global averages.</p><p>Containers often spend two weeks or more in many Sub-Saharan African ports before clearance, compared with just a few days in many of the world's leading ports.</p><p>Every additional day generates costs:</p><p>●Storage charges</p><p>●Demurrage</p><p>●Truck waiting fees</p><p>●Inventory carrying costs</p><p>●Lost production time</p><p>●Working capital tied up in delayed cargo</p><p>These hidden costs ripple across the supply chain, making African businesses—and ultimately African economies—less competitive.</p><p><br></p><p>3. <b>The Infrastructure Gap Is No Longer the Biggest Problem</b></p><p>The larger constraint today is operational efficiency.</p><p>Many ports continue to struggle with:</p><p>●Fragmented customs procedures</p><p>●Multiple government inspections</p><p>●Manual documentation</p><p>●Overlapping regulatory agencies</p><p>●Limited digital integration</p><p>●Poor coordination between public and private stakeholders</p><p>The outcome is predictable.</p><p>Ships wait.</p><p>Cargo waits.</p><p>Trucks wait.</p><p>Businesses pay.</p><p><br></p><p>4. <b>The Missing Link: Hinterland Connectivity</b></p><p>A modern port cannot compensate for weak transport networks.</p><p>Once cargo leaves the terminal, it must move efficiently to factories, warehouses, inland container depots, and neighbouring markets.</p><p><br></p><p>Across much of Africa, inadequate rail infrastructure and congested road networks continue to slow that movement.</p><p>In some countries, manufacturers located hundreds of kilometres inland spend several times more transporting goods from the port than businesses operating in comparable international markets.</p><p>Ports cannot be competitive if the corridors that connect them are not.</p><p><br></p><p>5. <b>When Congestion Becomes a Business Model</b></p><p>One of the least discussed challenges is that inefficiency can create financial incentives.</p><p>Extended cargo storage generates additional warehousing revenue.</p><p>Longer truck queues increase transport costs.</p><p>Multiple clearance stages create additional transaction costs.</p><p>Research from the Brookings Institution suggests that vested interests within parts of the logistics ecosystem can benefit from preserving inefficient systems rather than simplifying them.</p><p>This makes reform as much a governance challenge as an infrastructure challenge.</p><p><br></p><p>6. <b>The Next Phase Is Not Construction. It Is Capability.</b></p><p>Africa has demonstrated that it can finance and build modern ports.</p><p>The next competitive advantage will come from how efficiently those ports operate.</p><p>That requires:</p><p><ul><li>Digital single-window customs systems</li><li>Integrated port community systems</li><li>Stronger rail and inland logistics corridors</li><li>Transparent performance measurement</li><li>Better coordination among border agencies</li><li>Data-driven decision-making</li></ul></p><p>The ports that master these capabilities will attract more shipping services, reduce logistics costs, and strengthen national competitiveness.</p><p><br></p><h3>FINAL THOUGHT</h3><p>Africa does not have a port expansion problem.</p><p>It has an efficiency problem.</p><p>The next generation of competitive African ports will not be defined by the height of their cranes or the depth of their channels.</p><p>They will be defined by how quickly cargo moves from ship to market.</p><p>Because in global trade, value is not created when a vessel arrives.</p><p>Value is created when goods move efficiently through the entire supply chain.</p>
