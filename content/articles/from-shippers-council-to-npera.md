<h2>A New Architecture for Nigeria’s Port Economy</h2><p>Nigeria moves from an established regulatory foundation to a permanent statutory framework for economic regulation of its ports</p><p>Nigeria’s port sector is entering a new phase of economic regulation with the formal transition of the Nigerian Shippers’ Council into the Nigeria Ports Economic Regulatory Agency (NPERA), creating a permanent statutory framework for regulating the commercial environment of the country’s ports.</p><p>The transition follows President Bola Ahmed Tinubu’s assent to the Nigerian Ports Economic Regulatory Agency Act, 2026.</p><p>The development represents the latest stage in Nigeria’s efforts to strengthen economic regulation across its port system building on the regulatory functions and experience developed under the Nigerian Shippers’ Council while establishing a dedicated statutory agency with a broader and more clearly defined mandate.</p><p><img src="https://n0jyvquhgs2welj7.public.blob.vercel-storage.com/uploads/1787212610483-6aa1bff3-whatsapp-image-2026-08-20-at-08-12-38.webp" alt=""></p><p>The significance of the transition therefore goes beyond the creation of a new institution.</p><p>It represents an attempt to build a stronger regulatory architecture around one of the most important components of Nigeria’s trade and logistics economy.</p><p>Speaking at a press briefing in Lagos, Chairman of the NPERA Governing Board, Dr. Ibrahim Shema, CON, described the development as a major institutional reform aimed at creating a more transparent, predictable and competitive port environment.</p><p>Under its new mandate, NPERA will be responsible for the economic regulation of port services and related activities, including tariffs and charges, licensing, service standards, fair competition, commercial dispute resolution, trade facilitation and the protection of port users.</p><p>For a port economy in which the cost, speed and predictability of moving cargo directly affect the competitiveness of businesses, these functions place NPERA at the centre of some of Nigeria’s most important trade and logistics questions.</p><h3>From Shippers’ Council to NPERA</h3><p>The transition from the Nigerian Shippers’ Council to NPERA is more than an institutional rebranding.</p><p>The Nigerian Shippers’ Council has played a significant role in developing Nigeria’s port economic regulatory framework and in representing and protecting the interests of shippers and other port users.</p><p>Its evolution into NPERA therefore provides the new agency with something important: institutional experience.</p><p>The establishment of NPERA now provides that regulatory function with a dedicated statutory architecture and a clearer institutional foundation for economic regulation.</p><p>That distinction matters.</p><p>Effective economic regulation is not simply about issuing directives. It is about creating rules that investors, terminal operators, shipping lines, cargo owners, manufacturers, freight forwarders and other service providers can understand, anticipate and rely upon.</p><p>Dr. Shema said the new framework is expected to provide greater regulatory certainty for shipping lines and terminal operators, while giving importers, exporters, freight forwarders and clearing agents more predictable procedures, fairer charges and stronger mechanisms for resolving disputes.</p><p>The Agency’s stated regulatory principles transparency, fairness, predictability, efficiency and accountability therefore go directly to the commercial environment in which Nigeria’s ports operate.</p><p><img src="https://n0jyvquhgs2welj7.public.blob.vercel-storage.com/uploads/1787212543193-ea5fd943-whatsapp-image-2026-08-20-at-08-12-37.webp" alt=""><span style="color: rgb(21, 28, 46); font-family: &quot;Playfair Display&quot;, &quot;Playfair Display Fallback&quot;, Georgia, serif; font-size: 18px; font-weight: 600;">A Clearer Economic Regulatory Architecture</span></p><p>One of the important features of the new framework is the clarification of NPERA’s role within Nigeria’s wider port system.</p><p>Dr. Shema clarified that the establishment of NPERA does not create a competing authority with the Nigerian Ports Authority.</p><p>The Nigerian Ports Authority will retain responsibility for port infrastructure and its landlord functions, while NPERA will provide economic oversight within its statutory mandate.</p><p>That distinction could become one of the defining features of Nigeria’s emerging port regulatory architecture.</p><p>Nigeria’s port system involves infrastructure provision, terminal concessions, shipping services, cargo handling, tariffs, customs processes, maritime safety and a wide range of commercial relationships.</p><p>For the system to function efficiently, institutions must not only have clearly defined mandates; those mandates must also work together without creating duplication, conflicting requirements or regulatory uncertainty.</p><p>The new architecture therefore creates an opportunity for greater institutional clarity across the port ecosystem.</p><h3>The Tariff Question</h3><p>Among NPERA’s responsibilities, the regulation of tariffs and charges is likely to attract significant attention from port users.</p><p>Port costs have long been an important consideration for businesses operating within Nigeria’s trade and logistics ecosystem.</p><p>The new Agency has promised greater predictability and fairness.</p><p>For that promise to translate into economic value, however, businesses must be able to understand how charges are determined, reviewed and enforced.</p><p>Transparent processes for tariff administration could give importers, exporters, shipping companies, terminal operators and logistics businesses greater certainty when planning their operations.</p><p>The objective is not simply to regulate the level of charges.</p><p>It is to create a regulatory environment in which the commercial rules governing port services are sufficiently clear and predictable for businesses to make decisions with greater confidence.</p><p>That is one of the central opportunities presented by the new framework.</p><h3>Stronger Commercial Dispute Resolution&nbsp;</h3><p>Commercial dispute resolution is another area where NPERA could make a significant contribution.</p><p>The Agency’s Executive Secretary and Chief Executive Officer, Dr. Pius Akutah (MON), said the new Act would give NPERA stronger powers to improve commercial dispute resolution and protect the interests and welfare of port users and other stakeholders.</p><p>This is important because regulatory certainty is strengthened when businesses have access to credible mechanisms for resolving commercial disagreements.</p><p>A modern economic regulator should not only establish the rules of the market. It should also provide effective mechanisms for addressing disputes when those rules are contested.</p><p>If NPERA can develop a transparent, efficient and trusted dispute-resolution framework, it could strengthen confidence among port users, operators and investors.</p><h3>Technology and Data as Regulatory Infrastructure&nbsp;</h3><p>Another significant element of the Agency’s stated approach is its intention to deploy technology and data across licensing, tariff administration, monitoring, compliance, reporting and stakeholder engagement.</p><p>This could become one of the most important dimensions of the new regulatory framework.</p><p>Modern economic regulation increasingly depends on reliable data.</p><p>A regulator cannot effectively assess market behaviour, monitor service standards, understand tariff structures or evaluate compliance without access to accurate and timely information.</p><p>For NPERA, digitalisation should therefore go beyond simply moving regulatory processes online.</p><p>The larger opportunity is to build a data-driven regulatory system capable of identifying market trends, monitoring performance and providing a clearer picture of what is happening across Nigeria’s ports.</p><p>That could gradually move economic regulation from reactive intervention towards continuous market intelligence.</p><h3>Regulation Within a Wider Port Ecosystem</h3><p>NPERA will not operate in isolation.</p><p>The Agency plans to work with the Nigerian Ports Authority, NIMASA and the Nigeria Customs Service, alongside terminal operators, shipping lines, freight forwarders, manufacturers, investors and other stakeholders.</p><p>That cooperation will be critical.</p><p>Nigeria’s port performance is shaped by multiple institutions and processes. A reform in one part of the system can be weakened by inefficiencies elsewhere.</p><p>A more predictable tariff regime, for example, cannot by itself eliminate cargo delays.</p><p>Stronger commercial dispute resolution cannot solve infrastructure constraints.</p><p>Digital regulation cannot deliver its full potential if agencies continue to operate with disconnected systems and data.</p><p>NPERA’s emergence therefore creates an opportunity not only to strengthen economic regulation but also to encourage greater coherence across the institutions that shape Nigeria’s port and trade environment.</p><h3>From Framework to Implementation</h3><p>Dr. Shema said the immediate priority is to ensure an orderly transition from the Nigerian Shippers’ Council to NPERA while maintaining continuity in essential regulatory functions and preserving institutional knowledge.</p><p>That continuity is significant.</p><p>The new Agency is not starting from zero. It is building on an existing body of regulatory experience, stakeholder relationships and institutional knowledge developed over the years.</p><p>The next phase is to translate that foundation into the stronger statutory framework created by the NPERA Act.</p><p>The Executive Secretary and Chief Executive Officer, Dr. Pius Akutah, expressed optimism that the new law would significantly clarify the regulatory environment governing Nigeria’s ports within the next one or two years.</p><p>That points to an important reality: institutional reform is a process.</p><p>The passage of the Act and commencement of NPERA establish the framework. The economic impact will emerge through implementation.</p><p>For port users, the questions will ultimately be practical.</p><p>Will charges become more predictable?</p><p>Will commercial disputes be resolved more efficiently?</p><p>Will licensing become clearer?</p><p>Will service standards be measurable and enforceable?</p><p>Will competition between service providers improve?</p><p>Will investors have greater confidence in the stability of the regulatory environment?</p><p>And, ultimately, will the cost and uncertainty associated with doing business through Nigerian ports decline?</p><p>These are the measures by which the new regulatory architecture will eventually be judged.</p><p><img src="https://n0jyvquhgs2welj7.public.blob.vercel-storage.com/uploads/1787212629860-0d2544cb-whatsapp-image-2026-08-20-at-08-12-38-1.webp" alt=""></p><h2>PrimeAxis Insight&nbsp;</h2><p>The transition from the <b>Nigerian Shippers’ Council to NPERA</b> represents an important institutional development in Nigeria’s maritime economy.</p><p>Its significance lies not simply in the creation of a new agency, but in the opportunity to build on an existing regulatory foundation and give economic regulation a stronger and more permanent statutory architecture.</p><p>For Nigeria, this matters because ports are not merely gateways through which cargo enters and leaves the country.</p><p>They are part of the country’s economic infrastructure.</p><p>Shipping lines, terminal operators, manufacturers, importers, exporters, freight forwarders and investors all make decisions based on cost, time, risk and regulatory certainty.</p><p>When those variables are difficult to predict, they ultimately affect the competitiveness of the wider economy.</p><p>NPERA therefore has an opportunity to make predictability one of the defining features of Nigeria’s port economy.</p><p>Its success will depend on the quality of implementation: clear institutional boundaries, transparent tariff processes, credible enforcement, effective dispute resolution, reliable data and meaningful coordination among the agencies that shape the movement of cargo.</p><p>The transition also provides an opportunity to preserve and build upon the regulatory experience developed under the Nigerian Shippers’ Council while equipping the new agency with the statutory authority required for the next phase.</p><p>The Act has created the framework.</p><p>The task now is to turn that framework into a regulatory environment in which fair competition, efficient services, transparent charges and greater certainty become tangible features of doing business through Nigerian ports.</p><p>That is the promise of the new architecture.</p><p>And that is where the next chapter of Nigeria’s port economic regulation begins.</p>
