<h3>The industry demands visibility, ethical journalism and accountability. But what does it invest in the information ecosystem expected to deliver them?</h3><p>Nigeria wants a globally competitive maritime industry. It wants stronger indigenous shipping, efficient ports, maritime investment, better regulation, trade facilitation and a thriving blue economy. It also wants the public to understand these developments.</p><p>That requires more than press releases, event photographs and institutional social-media posts.</p><p>It requires specialist journalism capable of following policy beyond the announcement, interrogating claims, tracking implementation and preserving the institutional memory of an industry whose decisions affect trade, investment and national development.</p><p>Yet one question remains largely unasked:</p><p>Who pays to tell Nigeria’s maritime</p><p>This is not a request for charity.</p><p>It is a question about the economics of information.</p><h3>THE WARNING SIGNS ARE ALREADY THERE</h3><p>The economic vulnerability of Nigerian journalism is well documented.</p><p>A June 2026 Punch investigation examined poor remuneration, delayed salaries and weak welfare across Nigerian newsrooms, warning about the implications for journalism, accountability and ethics.</p><p>The broader problem extends beyond the maritime beat.</p><p>But maritime journalism has another layer of difficulty.</p><p>It is specialised.</p><p>It requires knowledge of shipping, ports, customs, cargo, logistics, maritime law, vessel operations, marine insurance, regulation, infrastructure and an increasingly complex blue economy.</p><p>It is also field-intensive.</p><p>The journalist has to move—to ports, conferences, regulators, courts, stakeholder meetings, vessels and terminals.</p><p>Research costs money.</p><p>Transportation costs money.</p><p>Data costs money.</p><p>Equipment costs money.</p><p>Production costs money.</p><p>Time costs money.</p><p>Specialist journalism is not cost-free.</p><p>Yet the value of producing it is often treated differently from the value of the industries it covers.</p><h3>THE INDUSTRY KNOWS THERE IS A PROBLEM</h3><p>This is what makes the conversation particularly important.</p><p>In February 2025, Comptroller-General of the Nigeria Customs Service, Bashir Adewale Adeniyi, publicly called for improved welfare for maritime journalists and raised the possibility of insurance and housing initiatives for those covering the sector. He also argued that journalists covering a growing maritime industry should have a pathway to better welfare.</p><p>The issue, therefore, is not invisible.</p><p>The industry has heard it.</p><p>Journalists have raised it.</p><p>Senior stakeholders have acknowledged it.</p><p>So the question becomes:</p><p>What has materially changed?</p><h3>WHAT DOES 20 YEARS OF MARITIME JOURNALISM BUY?</h3><p>Consider a journalist who has spent two decades covering Nigeria's maritime industry.</p><p>Twenty years of reporting port developments, regulatory reforms, shipping, customs, policy and trade.</p><p>Twenty years of interviewing CEOs, directors general, regulators and policymakers.</p><p>Twenty years of explaining an industry that wants to become globally competitive.</p><p>Now ask:</p><p>Can that journalist afford professional training without financial strain?</p><p>Can the journalist provide quality education for the children?</p><p>Can the family afford decent accommodation?</p><p>Is there meaningful health insurance?</p><p>What happens when illness comes?</p><p>What happens after retirement?</p><p>And if a journalist has spent 30 or 40 years covering the sector, why should international professional exposure still be financially out of reach?</p><p>There are Nigerian maritime journalists with decades of experience who struggle to secure sponsorship to cover major international maritime events.</p><p>That is not merely a media problem. It is a professional capacity problem for the industry itself.</p><p>A maritime sector cannot expect globally informed reporting while its specialist journalists remain disconnected from the international forums where global maritime policy, regulation and industry trends are shaped.</p><h3>WHY IS JOURNALISM EXPECTED TO BE FREE?</h3><p>This may be the most uncomfortable part of the conversation.</p><p>Nobody expects a lawyer to provide professional services because appearing in court gives the lawyer exposure.</p><p>Nobody expects an engineer to design infrastructure because the project will enhance the engineer's profile.</p><p>Nobody expects a consultant to provide months of strategic advice because the client is an important institution.</p><p>Yet journalism is routinely treated differently.</p><p>An invitation becomes compensation.</p><p>An interview becomes compensation.</p><p>Access becomes compensation.</p><p>Visibility becomes compensation.</p><p>But:</p><p>Access is not remuneration.</p><p>It does not pay salaries, insurance, transportation, equipment, data, rent or school</p><p>fees.</p><p>Journalism is a profession.</p><p>Its practitioners have the same economic obligations as other professionals.</p><p>So why is its professional value so frequently detached from the cost of producing it?</p><h3>WHERE IS THE MEDIA BUDGET?</h3><p>Every serious organisation communicates.</p><p>There are budgets for corporate communications, public relations, events,</p><p>branding, digital platforms, stakeholder engagement, publications, photography, video production, advertising and media buying.</p><p>Some organisations retain PR firms.</p><p>Some commission consultants.</p><p>Some spend significantly on events designed to generate visibility.</p><p>So the question is not whether money is being spent on communication.</p><p>The question is where that money goes.</p><p>How much is spent annually on:</p><p><ul><li>Public relations?</li><li>Events and publicity?</li><li>Advertising and media buying?</li><li>Digital communication?</li><li>Corporate publications?</li><li>Video production?</li><li>Stakeholder engagement?</li><li>External communications consultants?</li><li>Institutional campaigns?</li></ul></p><p>And critically:</p><p>How much reaches independent specialist maritime journalism?</p><p>That is the number worth examining.</p><p>Because there is a fundamental difference between paying to promote an institution and investing in the information ecosystem around an industry.</p><h3>VISIBILITY HAS A PRODUCTION COST</h3><p>A conference may require a venue, branding, protocol, logistics, production and</p><p>documentation.</p><p>Why is journalism often expected to appear as though it requires none?</p><p>A serious interview requires preparation, research, recording, editing, publishing and</p><p>distribution.</p><p>Investigative reporting requires even more.</p><p>A journalist who spends hours at a port or stakeholder meeting is not merely occupying a seat.</p><p>They are producing an information asset.</p><p>The audience sees the finished story.</p><p>The production cost remains invisible.</p><p>That invisibility is part of the problem.</p><h3>ETHICS CANNOT BE SEPARATED FROM ECONOMICS</h3><p>The maritime industry rightly demands ethical journalism.</p><p>Journalists should not be bought.</p><p>They should not manufacture stories, distort facts, blackmail institutions or compromise professional independence. But there is an uncomfortable contradiction in demanding uncompromising ethics while ignoring the economic vulnerability of the profession.</p><p>Financial insecurity does not automatically produce unethical journalism.</p><p>But it creates vulnerability.</p><p>Nigeria's wider media sector has already raised concerns about the relationship between poor welfare and ethical pressure.</p><p>This makes journalist welfare more than an employment issue.</p><p>It is an integrity issue.</p><h3>THE MEDIA MUST ALSO LOOK INWARD</h3><p>The answer cannot simply be: <b>“The industry should pay us.”</b></p><p>Maritime media must become better businesses.</p><p>Models, research products, intelligence services, premium analysis, subscriptions, data products and credible commercial metrics.</p><p>The profession also needs stronger collaboration and collective standards.</p><p>In 2025, the Customs CG publicly criticised fragmentation among maritime media</p><p>associations and argued that greater unity could improve access to welfare opportunities, training, insurance and housing initiatives.</p><p>The media therefore has work to do.</p><p>But commercial innovation does not remove the responsibility of the industry to recognise the value of the journalism it consumes.</p><h3>WHAT HAPPENS WHEN SPECIALIST JOURNALISM DISAPPEARS?</h3><p>This is the question the maritime industry should worry about.</p><p>A veteran maritime journalist possesses something that cannot be acquired overnight:</p><p>Institutional memory.</p><p>They remember the previous reform.</p><p>The previous promise.</p><p>The previous policy.</p><p>What was implemented.</p><p>What disappeared after the headlines.</p><p>Which questions remain unanswered.</p><p>That knowledge matters.</p><p>When experienced journalists leave because the profession cannot sustain them, the industry loses more than reporters.</p><p>It loses continuity.</p><p>It loses expertise.</p><p>It loses scrutiny.</p><p>And eventually, it risks becoming increasingly dependent on information supplied by the institutions being covered.</p><p>That should concern everyone.</p><h3>THE MARITIME MEDIA IS PART OF THE INDUSTRY'S INFORMATION INFRASTRUCTURE.</h3><p>Ships require infrastructure.</p><p>Ports require infrastructure.</p><p>Trade requires infrastructure.</p><p>A modern maritime economy also requires information infrastructure.</p><p>Reliable data.</p><p>Research.</p><p>Analysis.</p><p>Independent journalism.</p><p>Technical knowledge.</p><p>Institutional memory.</p><p>Public scrutiny.</p><p>These are not decorative additions to an industry.</p><p>They are part of the ecosystem that allows an industry to understand itself and be held accountable.</p><p>If that ecosystem becomes economically unsustainable, the consequences extend beyond journalists.</p><h3>SO, WHO PAYS?</h3><p>The question is deliberately uncomfortable.</p><p>Not:</p><p>“Why should we pay journalists?”</p><p>But:</p><p>What does Nigeria's maritime industry spend on communication—and what portion of that investment sustains independent journalism?</p><p>If communications budgets exist but are concentrated elsewhere, we need to understand why.</p><p>If organisations genuinely have limited resources, then we need to confront how specialist media can survive in an industry that increasingly depends on its visibility.</p><p>And if the industry wants better journalism, it must recognise that better journalism requires an economic foundation.</p><h3>THIS IS NOT ABOUT CHARITY</h3><p>Maritime journalists do not need pity.</p><p>They need a viable professional ecosystem.</p><p>Media organisations need sustainable business models.</p><p>Journalists need decent remuneration and professional development.</p><p>Specialist publications need legitimate commercial opportunities.</p><p>And the industry needs independent media capable of asking difficult questions without becoming financially dependent on the institutions it covers.</p><p>The relationship must move from favour to value.</p><h3>THE QUESTION NIGERIA'S MARITIME INDUSTRY SHOULD NOW ANSWER</h3><p>Nigeria wants more ships.</p><p>More ports.</p><p>More cargo.</p><p>More indigenous capacity.</p><p>More maritime finance.</p><p>More blue-economy investment.</p><p>More international competitiveness.</p><p>All of that requires knowledge.</p><p>And knowledge requires institutions capable of producing and interrogating it.</p><p>Journalism is one of those institutions.</p><p>A journalist should not spend 20, 30 or 40 years explaining an international industry and remain unable to afford professional development, decent housing, quality education for the family or basic financial protection.</p><p>That is not a sustainable profession.</p><p>And ultimately, it is not a sustainable information ecosystem.</p><p>The maritime media must change.</p><p>The industry must change.</p><p>The business model must change.</p><p>But the conversation can no longer be avoided.</p><p>Because an industry cannot demand visibility,</p><p>ethical journalism and accountability while allowing the professional ecosystem for delivering them to become economically unsustainable.</p><p>So the question is no longer simply whether maritime media deserves to survive.</p><p>It is:</p><h3>WHO PAYS TO TELL NIGERIA'S MARITIME STORY?</h3><p>And beneath that question lies the one the industry may find harder to answer:</p><h3>Where is the media budget and where is the money going?</h3>
