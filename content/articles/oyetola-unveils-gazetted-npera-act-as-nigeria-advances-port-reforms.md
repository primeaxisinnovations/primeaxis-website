<p class="MsoNormal">The Minister of Marine and Blue Economy, Dr Adegboyega
Oyetola, has unveiled the gazetted Nigeria Ports Economic Regulatory Agency
(NPERA) Act, 2026, alongside the agency’s official logo, marking a significant
step in Nigeria’s efforts to strengthen port economic regulation, attract
investment and improve maritime competitiveness.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">The unveiling took place on Friday, 16 October 2026, at the
Federal Ministry of Marine and Blue Economy’s Third Quarter 2026 Citizens’ and
Stakeholders’ Engagement in Victoria Island, Lagos.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">The development follows President Bola Ahmed Tinubu’s assent
to the Act in June 2026, establishing a substantive statutory framework for port
economic regulation after nearly two decades of efforts to institutionalise the
function.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">Oyetola disclosed that he had directed the immediate
constitution of a ministerial committee to oversee the transition of the
Nigerian Shippers’ Council, which has served as interim port economic regulator
since 2014, into NPERA.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">Under the new framework, the agency will regulate tariffs
and charges, promote competition, enforce service standards, resolve commercial
disputes and protect port users’ interests.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">The Minister emphasised that NPERA must operate as an
impartial regulator, free from competing responsibilities that could compromise
its independence or create actual or perceived conflicts of interest.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">The announcement formed part of a wider update on the government’s
port reform agenda, covering infrastructure modernisation, deep seaport
development, indigenous shipping, maritime security and international
cooperation.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">The engagement, themed “Building Strategic Bilateral
Partnerships for Sustainable Growth, Investment, and Technology of Nigeria’s
Marine and Blue Economy,” brought together government officials, industry
leaders, development partners and other stakeholders to discuss investment
opportunities and sectoral reforms.<o:p></o:p></p>

<h2>ESTABLISHING CREDIBLE PORT ECONOMIC REGULATION<o:p></o:p></h2>

<p class="MsoNormal">The transition to NPERA represents more than a change in
institutional identity. It establishes a dedicated statutory framework for
regulating the commercial conditions under which Nigeria’s ports operate.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">For port users, shipping companies, terminal operators and
logistics providers, the agency’s mandate touches some of the most
consequential aspects of doing business: tariffs, charges, service standards,
competition and the resolution of commercial disputes.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">Oyetola’s emphasis on independence is particularly
significant. An economic regulator must be able to assess commercial practices
objectively and enforce its mandate without conflicting institutional interests
undermining its decisions.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">However, the effectiveness of this arrangement will depend
on how that independence is secured in practice, how responsibilities are
defined and how consistently regulatory powers are exercised.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">The ministerial committee overseeing the transition
therefore has an important task. Moving the Nigerian Shippers’ Council’s
interim regulatory functions into the new agency must preserve continuity while
establishing the operational structures needed for effective oversight.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">For investors, statutory authority is an important
foundation, but confidence also depends on predictable rules, transparent
decisions and credible dispute resolution.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">NPERA’s establishment creates the framework. Its
implementation will determine whether that framework improves the commercial
environment across Nigeria’s ports.<o:p></o:p></p>

<h2>PORT EXPANSION AND THE COMPETITIVENESS QUESTION<o:p></o:p></h2>

<p class="MsoNormal">The Minister reaffirmed the government’s commitment to
modernising Apapa, Tin Can Island, Onne, Rivers, Delta and Calabar ports,
stressing that the programme would promote balanced development across the
country.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">He also disclosed that the Federal Government had approved
the development of the Ibom, Bakassi, Agge, Gateway, Ondo and Bonny Deep
Seaports to expand maritime capacity and strengthen Nigeria’s position as a
regional logistics hub.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">The commitment to ports beyond Lagos is important to a
national trade strategy. Nigeria’s port competitiveness cannot rest solely on
the performance of its principal Lagos facilities while other maritime gateways
remain central to regional economic development.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">Nevertheless, infrastructure expansion does not
automatically translate into greater competitiveness.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">The economic value of port investment depends on whether
facilities attract sustainable cargo volumes, connect efficiently with inland
markets, offer commercially viable services and reduce the cost and uncertainty
of moving goods.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">This is where infrastructure policy and economic regulation
intersect. Modernisation can improve physical capacity, while effective
regulation can promote transparency in charges, fair competition and adherence
to service standards.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">The combined objective should be a port system that is not
only larger, but more efficient, predictable and attractive to businesses.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">The government's commitment to balanced development will
therefore need to be demonstrated through implementation and measurable
improvements across the national port network.<o:p></o:p></p>

<h2>SHIPPING CAPACITY, SECURITY AND INTERNATIONAL PARTNERSHIPS<o:p></o:p></h2>

<p class="MsoNormal">Oyetola also reaffirmed the government’s commitment to
indigenous shipping, disclosing that he had directed the Nigerian Maritime
Administration and Safety Agency (NIMASA) to work with approved Primary Lending
Institutions to accelerate the processing and disbursement of the Cabotage
Vessel Financing Fund to qualified applicants.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">The directive brings attention to the practical challenge of
converting an established financing mechanism into greater indigenous
participation in vessel ownership and maritime transportation.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">For Nigerian operators, the key outcome is access to funding
that enables qualified applicants to acquire or develop vessel capacity. Faster
processing and actual disbursement, rather than administrative commitments
alone, will determine the fund’s contribution to domestic shipping development.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">On maritime security, the Minister stated that Nigeria had
maintained zero piracy incidents in its territorial waters through the Deep
Blue Project. He also described the lifting of the 12 year United States Coast
Guard Condition of Entry on vessels arriving from Nigeria in August 2026 as a
major achievement.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">According to the Minister, the development reflected
progress in maritime security and compliance with the International Ship and
Port Facility Security Code.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">These achievements are relevant to Nigeria’s standing in
international shipping. Sustaining that confidence, however, requires continued
security performance and consistent compliance with international standards.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">International cooperation was another major theme of the
engagement. Oyetola cited Nigeria’s election to Category C of the International
Maritime Organization Council in November 2025 and ongoing engagements with
Denmark, India, Norway and the United Kingdom.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">The Ambassador of the Kingdom of Denmark to Nigeria, Jens
Ole Bach Hansen, presented Denmark’s experience in building a sustainable blue
economy, with lessons for strategic partnerships, investment and technology
transfer.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">The Minister stressed that international partnerships must
strengthen local capabilities and deliver measurable, sustainable outcomes.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">That principle is central to the value of such cooperation.
Partnerships should translate into practical gains in technology, institutional
capacity, investment and expertise rather than remain limited to diplomatic
engagement.<o:p></o:p></p>

<h2>PRIMEAXIS INSIGHT<o:p></o:p></h2>

<p class="MsoNormal">The unveiling of the NPERA Act and logo brings Nigeria’s
port economic regulation into a new institutional phase. The larger question is
whether the reform will change the conditions under which businesses use and
invest in the country’s ports.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">Three priorities stand out: an effective transition to the
new regulator, credible enforcement of transparent commercial rules and a port
modernisation programme that delivers measurable improvements in efficiency and
competitiveness.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">These priorities are interdependent. Infrastructure
investment cannot reach its full economic potential if commercial practices
remain unpredictable. Regulatory authority cannot build investor confidence
without independence, consistency and effective implementation. Indigenous
shipping cannot expand sustainably without meaningful access to finance.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">The same standard applies to international partnerships.
Their value must be demonstrated through stronger domestic capabilities and
tangible economic outcomes.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">For NPERA, the immediate task is to turn statutory authority
into credible oversight. That means ensuring that charges are transparent,
service standards are observed, disputes are resolved effectively and
businesses understand the rules governing their operations.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">For the government, the broader challenge is to connect
regulatory reform, infrastructure investment, shipping finance and maritime
security within a coherent competitiveness agenda.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">Nigeria has taken an important legislative step. The next
measure of progress will be whether port users experience a more predictable
commercial environment, investors gain greater confidence in the regulatory
system and the national port network becomes more efficient.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">The success of the reform will not be determined by the
establishment of another agency, but by the economic value its regulation
delivers to Nigeria’s ports and the businesses that depend on them.<o:p></o:p></p>
