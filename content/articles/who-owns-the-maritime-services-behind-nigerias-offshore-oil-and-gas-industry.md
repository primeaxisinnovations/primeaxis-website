<p>Nigeria’s offshore oil and gas industry is supported by a maritime services network that rarely receives the same attention as the production figures it helps sustain.&nbsp;</p><p>Offshore support vessels, platform supply vessels, anchor handling tug supply vessels, crew boats, subsea support vessels and other specialist assets are essential to exploration, production and offshore logistics.</p><p>Yet an important question remains: how much of this business is genuinely owned, controlled and retained by Nigerian companies?</p><p>The issue goes beyond the number of vessels operating in Nigerian waters. It concerns who owns the assets, who finances them, who controls their deployment, who provides the technical services and, ultimately, where the economic value generated by offshore activity resides.</p><p>For a country seeking to deepen indigenous participation in its oil and gas industry, these questions are central to the future of its maritime economy.</p><h2>THE OWNERSHIP QUESTION</h2><p>Nigeria has made measurable progress in developing indigenous participation in marine services. The Nigerian Content Development and Monitoring Board (NCDMB), through its Marine Vessels Scheme and wider local content framework, has sought to encourage Nigerian ownership, construction and utilisation of vessels supporting the oil and gas industry.</p><p>According to NCDMB’s 2025 Transformational Achievements report, Nigerian owned vessels operating in the oil and gas industry increased from less than 10 per cent in 2010 to 37.1 per cent in 2021 and 47.34 per cent in 2025.</p><p>The report also recorded 828 approved marine vessel records, comprising 392 Nigerian built and 436 foreign built vessels.</p><p>These figures indicate progress, but they measure different aspects of the industry. The percentage of Nigerian owned vessels is an ownership measure, while the number of Nigerian built vessels reflects domestic construction capacity.&nbsp;</p><p>Neither figure, on its own, establishes how many vessels are actively deployed, the scale of their contracts or the value retained by Nigerian operators.</p><p>The distinction matters. A vessel may be Nigerian owned but foreign built. A Nigerian company may own an asset but depend on foreign financing, specialist maintenance, technical management or operational support.</p><p>The real test is whether Nigerian companies can build, finance, operate, maintain and compete with the assets required by the offshore industry.</p><h2>LOCAL CONTENT AND THE LIMITS OF OWNERSHIP</h2><p>The Nigerian Oil and Gas Industry Content Development Act 2010 established a framework for increasing Nigerian participation in the oil and gas value chain. NCDMB's Marine Vessels Scheme supports this objective by promoting Nigerian ownership and the utilisation of vessels built in Nigeria.</p><p>The Coastal and Inland Shipping (Cabotage) Act 2003 also provides a framework for reserving specified domestic shipping activities for Nigerian participation, subject to its provisions and applicable waivers.</p><p>Together, these frameworks create opportunities for indigenous maritime businesses. But legislation does not automatically create competitive companies.</p><p>Operators still need access to long term finance, predictable contracts, technical expertise, insurance, classification services, maintenance facilities and a sufficiently strong market to sustain expensive marine assets.</p><p>Offshore vessels are capital intensive. Their acquisition and operation require substantial investment, while contracts must be commercially viable enough to support debt repayment and continuing maintenance.&nbsp;</p><p>A company may acquire a vessel but struggle to keep it productively employed if it cannot secure contracts or compete with operators backed by stronger international financing arrangements.</p><p>This is where the gap between policy ambition and commercial reality becomes visible.</p><h2>THE FINANCING QUESTION: WHAT HAPPENED TO THE CVFF?</h2><p>Few issues illustrate the financing challenge more clearly than the Cabotage Vessel Financing Fund (CVFF).</p><p>Established under the Coastal and Inland Shipping (Cabotage) Act 2003, the fund was intended to provide financial support to eligible Nigerian shipowners for vessel acquisition and strengthen indigenous participation in domestic shipping.</p><p><br></p><p>Although the CVFF is not specifically an offshore oil and gas financing scheme, its accessibility has implications for the wider indigenous shipping industry, including the capacity of Nigerian operators to acquire and sustain vessels for maritime services.</p><p>For years, the fund remained largely inaccessible to the operators it was designed to support. Its movement towards implementation has therefore attracted considerable attention.</p><p>In January 2026, an application portal was launched to enable eligible shipowners to seek access to the facility. By September, 92 applications had reportedly been received, with 20 forwarded to participating Primary Lending Institutions (PLIs). Only one application had completed the reported assessment process and been forwarded for ministerial approval.</p><p>These figures raised questions about the pace of implementation. They also exposed the difficulties of translating an established financing facility into actual access to capital.</p><p>The lending process has generated further concerns. Shipowners have reported difficulties meeting lenders' requirements, including requests for cargo guarantees and collateral.</p><p>The commercial dilemma is evident: operators need vessels to compete for cargo, but may need evidence of cargo commitments to secure the financing required to acquire those vessels.</p><p>The government subsequently announced a major step towards disbursement. On October 29, 2026, the Minister of Marine and Blue Economy, Adegboyega Oyetola, announced that the Federal Government had begun disbursing the CVFF to Nigerian shipowners.</p><p>However, the announcement raises an important question about the actual stage of implementation. The Ministry's subsequent communication on October 30 called for an acceleration of the disbursement process, while NIMASA's November 1 statement described disbursement as imminent and disclosed that some applicants were still completing documentation with their preferred lending institutions.</p><p>The publicly reported application figures have also continued to feature in accounts of the implementation process. The announcement of disbursement is significant, but stakeholders still need clear information on completed transactions, beneficiary companies, amounts approved and released, and the vessels expected to be acquired.</p><p>The success of the CVFF should ultimately be measured by actual financing reaching qualified operators and translating into productive maritime assets.</p><h2>A NEW CONTROVERSY OVER THE FUND</h2><p>The financing debate has now been joined by a more fundamental question about the CVFF's financial history and currency management.</p><p>Former NIMASA Director General, Temisan Omatseye, who led the agency from July 2009 to December 2010, said dollar denominated contributions were converted into naira during his tenure following a directive from the Office of the Accountant General of the Federation.</p><p>He explained that the decision was influenced by the higher interest rates available on naira deposits at the time.</p><p>Omatseye also said that when he left office in December 2010, NIMASA had ₦52 billion in fixed deposits. Importantly, he clarified that this figure comprised both the CVFF and the Maritime Fund, not the CVFF alone.</p><p>He further stated that the practice following the introduction of the Treasury Single Account was for NIMASA to explain.</p><p>The current NIMASA Director General, Dr Dayo Mobereola, has rejected the allegation as a description of the fund's present status.</p><p>In a statement issued on November 1 and reported on November 2, the agency maintained that dollar denominated contributions are retained in dollars with the Central Bank of Nigeria, while naira contributions remain in naira. It said there had been no conversion of the dollar component, contrary to the allegation.</p><p>These accounts relate to different periods of agency management and should not be treated as though they describe the same administrative period. Omatseye has spoken about what he says happened during his tenure, while NIMASA has stated how it says the fund is currently held.</p><p>What remains necessary is a documented financial history that reconciles contributions, currency treatment, investments, interest earnings and subsequent transfers.</p><p>The controversy intensified on November 3 when the Sea Empowerment and Research Centre (SEREC) renewed its call for an independent forensic audit of NIMASA's accounts from 2004 to date.</p><p>The organisation argued that the competing accounts made a comprehensive examination necessary, including the fund's contributions, currency conversions, investments, accrued interest and administrative transactions.</p><p>Public estimates of the fund's accumulated value have also varied, with figures of approximately $350 million and $700 million appearing in different discussions. Neither figure should be treated as an independently reconciled current balance.</p><p>An independent examination would help establish what has accrued, what has been invested, what remains available and what has been disbursed.</p><p>The issue is not simply whether the fund is held in dollars or naira. It is whether its complete financial history can be demonstrated through records that stakeholders and the public can examine.</p><h2>BEYOND THE VESSELS</h2><p>Nigeria's offshore maritime services industry cannot be assessed only by counting vessels or measuring the percentage of assets classified as Nigerian owned.</p><p>The country must also examine the depth of its indigenous capabilities across vessel management, marine engineering, ship repair, subsea services, specialist equipment, crewing, insurance and technical support.</p><p>The objective should be to move Nigerian companies beyond participation at the margins of offshore operations towards stronger ownership and control of the services that sustain them.</p><p>That requires coordination between NCDMB, NIMASA, the Federal Ministry of Marine and Blue Economy and the relevant oil and gas institutions. It also requires financing arrangements that recognise the long term nature of shipping investments and procurement practices that create genuine opportunities for capable indigenous operators.</p><p>The CVFF must be part of that conversation, but access to financing alone will not solve every structural weakness. The industry needs transparent fund management, commercially sound lending, enforceable local content commitments and a stronger domestic maritime services base.</p><h2>PRIMEAXIS INSIGHT</h2><p>Nigeria's offshore oil and gas industry generates opportunities far beyond the production platform. Every operation depends on a network of vessels, people, technology, finance and specialised maritime services.</p><p>The question is whether Nigerian businesses are positioned to own and control a meaningful share of that value chain, or whether the country will continue to rely heavily on external capacity to support an industry operating within its waters.</p><p>The progress recorded in indigenous vessel ownership is significant. But ownership figures must translate into productive deployment, technical competence, sustainable businesses and greater domestic value retention.</p><p>Equally important, public financing intended to support this transition must deliver measurable results. The CVFF must be accounted for, its financial position established and its disbursement process made transparent and credible.</p><p>Nigeria's offshore industry should not only produce oil and gas. It should also build a competitive maritime services industry capable of supporting that production, creating indigenous capacity and retaining more of its economic value at home.</p>
