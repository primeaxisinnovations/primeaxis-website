<h2>Industrial Capability</h2><p>Africa wants to own more ships.</p><p>Governments are strengthening cabotage policies.</p><p>Regional trade is expanding.</p><p>The African Continental Free Trade Area (AfCFTA) is creating new opportunities for intra-African shipping.</p><p>Yet one critical question remains largely unanswered:</p><p>Who will build the ships?</p><p>And just as importantly—</p><p>Who will repair them?</p><p>Because maritime ambition cannot be sustained without industrial capability.</p><p>A nation that cannot build, maintain, or repair its vessels will remain dependent on others, regardless of how many ships fly its flag.</p><h3>1. Shipping Requires More Than Ship Ownership</h3><p>Across Africa, conversations about maritime development often focus on increasing indigenous ship ownership.</p><p>That ambition is important.</p><p>But ownership is only one part of the equation.</p><p>A competitive maritime industry requires an integrated industrial ecosystem that includes shipbuilding, dry-docking, repairs, maintenance, marine engineering, component manufacturing, and specialised technical services.</p><p>Without that ecosystem, ship ownership alone creates limited domestic value.</p><h3>2. Africa Captures Only a Fraction of a Global Industry</h3><p>The global shipbuilding market is projected to exceed US$270 billion over the coming decade.</p><p>Yet Africa captures only a tiny share of that value.</p><p>Despite the continent's extensive coastline, strategic location, growing ports, and expanding maritime trade, most commercial vessels operating in African waters are built, maintained, and repaired outside the continent.</p><p>Every vessel sent abroad for repairs represents more than a maintenance bill.</p><p>It represents lost industrial output.</p><p>Lost jobs.</p><p>Lost technical expertise.</p><p>Lost foreign exchange.</p><p>Lost opportunities to build local capability.</p><h3>3. The Cost of Industrial Dependence</h3><p>Across much of Africa, shipowners rely on foreign shipyards for major repairs, dry-docking, and specialised maintenance because local capacity remains limited.</p><p>This dependence comes at a cost.</p><p>Longer vessel downtime.</p><p>Higher maintenance costs.</p><p>Foreign exchange outflows.</p><p>Limited technology transfer.</p><p>Reduced competitiveness for indigenous operators.</p><p>The result is a cycle in which Africa generates maritime activity but exports much of the industrial value associated with it.</p><h3>4. Shipbuilding Is an Industrial Ecosystem</h3><p>A successful shipbuilding industry is not built around shipyards alone.</p><p>It depends on a broader manufacturing and engineering ecosystem.</p><p>That ecosystem includes:</p><p><ul><li>Modern dry docks and repair yards</li><li>Steel production</li><li>Marine equipment manufacturing</li><li>Engine and propulsion systems</li><li>Precision engineering</li><li>Welding and fabrication</li><li>Naval architecture</li><li>Marine engineering</li><li>Classification and certification services</li><li>Research, development, and innovation</li></ul></p><p>Shipbuilding is therefore not simply a maritime industry.</p><p>It is an industrial development strategy.</p><h3>5. The Human Capital Challenge</h3><p>Industrial capability depends on people.</p><p>Africa needs more naval architects.</p><p>More marine engineers.</p><p>More welders certified to international standards.</p><p>More marine surveyors.</p><p>More ship designers.</p><p>More production engineers.</p><p>More specialists in automation, digital ship design, and advanced manufacturing.</p><p>Infrastructure can be financed.</p><p>Technology can be imported.</p><p>Industrial capability must be built.</p><h3>6. AfCFTA Creates a New Opportunity</h3><p>The African Continental Free Trade Area offers an opportunity to rethink how Africa develops its maritime industries.</p><p>Rather than each country attempting to build a complete shipbuilding ecosystem independently, regional value chains can distribute capability across the continent.</p><p>Steel from one country.</p><p>Marine equipment from another.</p><p>Shipbuilding in specialised regional yards.</p><p>Repairs supported by integrated logistics corridors.</p><p>This is how globally competitive industrial ecosystems are built.</p><h3>7. From Maritime Consumers to Maritime Producers</h3><p>For decades, Africa has largely participated in the maritime economy as a consumer of ships and maritime services.</p><p>The next phase must be different.</p><p>The continent must increasingly become a producer.</p><p>Not only of ships—</p><p>But of maritime technology.</p><p>Industrial expertise.</p><p>Engineering capability.</p><p>Innovation.</p><p>And skilled employment.</p><p>That is where long-term value is created.</p><h2>FINAL THOUGHT</h2><p>Africa's indigenous shipping ambition cannot end with owning more vessels.</p><p>It must extend to building them.</p><p>Maintaining them.</p><p>Repairing them.</p><p>And developing the industries that support them.</p><p>Because a competitive maritime nation is not defined only by the number of ships it owns.</p><p>It is defined by its ability to design, build, maintain, repair, and continuously improve them.</p><p>Industrial capability is the missing link between Africa's maritime ambition and true maritime independence.</p>
