<p class="MsoNormal">Nigeria’s effort to establish a regional
register of authorised fishing vessels has reached a stage where the quality of
implementation matters as much as the ambition behind it. The initiative is
intended to improve transparency, strengthen compliance and help authorities
identify vessels legally permitted to operate across the West Central Gulf of
Guinea. But a critical finding from Nigeria’s pilot phase raises questions
about the records, verification procedures and institutional coordination on
which the system will depend.</p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">In August 2026, the Fisheries Committee for
the West Central Gulf of Guinea (FCWC) disclosed that testing the proposed
Regional Record of Authorised Fishing Vessels (RRAFV) against Nigeria’s fishing
fleet revealed practical difficulties in applying some agreed eligibility
criteria. The criteria were subsequently refined, and a draft protocol was
prepared to formalise the register.</span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">That finding deserves closer attention. If
the criteria proved difficult to apply to Nigeria’s fleet, what precisely
caused the difficulty? Were the challenges related to incomplete documentation,
inconsistent records, vessel ownership information or the interpretation of
eligibility requirements? And what changed during the pilot to make the
proposed system workable?<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">&nbsp;</span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">The FCWC has reported that the criteria
were refined, but the public needs a clearer account of the problems
encountered and how the revisions address them. The register is expected to
begin registration in 2027. Before that deadline, the institutions responsible
must demonstrate that the system can produce reliable information and support
effective enforcement.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<h2><span lang="EN-US">THE DATA PROBLEM BEHIND FISHERIES
ENFORCEMENT<o:p></o:p></span></h2>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">A vessel register is only as reliable as
the information it contains. The FCWC’s Regional Plan of Action against
illegal, unreported and unregulated fishing identifies weaknesses in fisheries
governance, including inconsistencies between fisheries and merchant shipping
records, changes in vessel ownership that are not reported to maritime
authorities, and shortcomings in the maintenance and updating of vessel
databases.</span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">These are not minor administrative
concerns. A vessel may appear in one record but not another.</span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">Ownership information may no longer reflect
the actual interests behind its operation. Documents may be incomplete or
difficult to authenticate.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">Where authorities cannot confidently
establish a vessel’s identity, ownership and authorisation status, monitoring
and enforcement become more complicated.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">The regional register is an opportunity to
address these weaknesses, but it must do more than consolidate existing
records. It must establish a dependable basis for determining which vessels
qualify for registration and whether their information remains accurate over
time.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">&nbsp;</span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">Three questions are central. What specific
eligibility criteria proved difficult to apply during Nigeria’s pilot? What
documentary and ownership checks will be required before a vessel is admitted
to the register? And how will the responsible authorities resolve conflicting
information between fisheries records, vessel registration documents and other
official databases?<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">These questions matter because the
credibility of the register will depend on verification, not simply the number
of vessels entered into the system. Authorities must also establish how changes
in ownership, authorisation and vessel status will be recorded, who will be
responsible for updating the information, and how errors will be corrected.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">Without clear procedures, a register can
reproduce the weaknesses it was created to address.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<h2><span lang="EN-US">THE 2027 DEADLINE AND THE IMPLEMENTATION TEST<o:p></o:p></span></h2>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">At a sensitisation workshop held in Lagos
on 22 and 23 June 2026, the FCWC and Nigeria’s fisheries authorities discussed
the proposed registration process and its implications for vessel operators.
Preparatory work includes analysing vessels licensed to fish in Nigerian waters
in 2026.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">The FCWC stated that registration is
expected to begin in 2027. Industrial fishing vessels operating in Nigeria are
to be included without a registration fee through the end of 2028. A five per
cent levy on the national authorisation fee, payable in naira, is intended to
support the operations of the Regional Monitoring, Control and Surveillance
Centre through a dedicated platform.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">These arrangements establish a timetable
and a proposed funding mechanism. The next test is whether the necessary
groundwork will be completed in time.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">How many vessels have been assessed against
the proposed requirements, and how many have had their records verified? When
will the revised eligibility criteria and final protocol be made available?
What is the process for handling vessels whose documentation is incomplete or
whose records conflict across government agencies?<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">&nbsp;</span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">Institutional responsibility is equally
important. Nigeria’s fisheries authorities, the Nigerian Navy, NIMASA and other
relevant institutions have distinct roles in fisheries administration, vessel
oversight and maritime enforcement. The register will require coordination
among these bodies, but coordination must be translated into clear
responsibilities.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">Which institution will validate the
information submitted by vessel operators? Who will make the final decision on
eligibility? Who will be accountable for the accuracy of each entry? And what
procedure will apply when one agency’s records contradict another’s?<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">The funding arrangement also requires
transparency. How will the five per cent levy be collected, administered and
accounted for? What specific monitoring and enforcement capabilities will it
finance, and how will the public assess whether those resources are producing
results?<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">These are practical implementation
questions, not objections to the initiative. Answering them before registration
begins would help vessel operators understand their obligations while giving
the public a basis for assessing progress.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<h2><span lang="EN-US">A REGISTER MUST CONNECT TO ENFORCEMENT<o:p></o:p></span></h2>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">The need for reliable vessel information is
underscored by the FCWC’s regional enforcement work.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">In a report published on 2 September 2026,
the FCWC said its Regional Monitoring, Control and Surveillance Centre
coordinated a simultaneous fisheries patrol involving Nigeria and Liberia from
26 to 28 August. Fisheries monitoring personnel, inspectors and naval and coast
guard personnel from the two countries worked through the regional centre in
Tema, Ghana, with real time intelligence and analytical support.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">According to the FCWC, Nigerian authorities
arrested three vessels for fisheries violations, while Liberian authorities
boarded a suspected cargo vessel for further inspection. The operation was
designed to strengthen coordination, information sharing and enforcement
against illegal, unreported and unregulated fishing.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">The operation demonstrates the value of
regional cooperation. It also points to the next accountability test: whether
authorities can reliably establish a vessel’s identity, ownership and
authorisation status when potential violations are detected.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">How will information from the regional
register be used during inspections and patrols? How will authorities verify a
vessel’s status when it operates across national jurisdictions? And how will
enforcement outcomes be tracked, from an initial inspection or arrest through
investigation, prosecution and any eventual sanctions?<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">A register cannot replace effective
enforcement.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">&nbsp;</span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">Its value will be measured by whether it
helps authorities identify non compliant vessels, share credible information,
act on violations and demonstrate results.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">The distinction is important. Registration
figures alone will not establish whether illegal fishing is being deterred or
whether fisheries governance is improving. Authorities will need to show how
the register informs operational decisions and contributes to measurable
enforcement outcomes.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<h2><span lang="EN-US">PRIMEAXIS INSIGHT<o:p></o:p></span></h2>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">Nigeria’s pilot has exposed a practical
challenge at the heart of the proposed Regional Record of Authorised Fishing
Vessels: agreeing on eligibility criteria is one thing; applying them
consistently to a real fleet is another.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">&nbsp;</span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">The FCWC has confirmed that the criteria
were refined and a draft protocol prepared. The next stage requires greater
clarity on what the pilot revealed, how vessel information will be verified,
which institutions will be accountable for maintaining the register, and how
the system will support enforcement when registration begins in 2027.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">&nbsp;</span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">The questions are straightforward. What
changed in the eligibility criteria after Nigeria’s pilot? How much of the
fleet has been verified? Who is responsible for the accuracy of the records?
And what measurable enforcement improvements should Nigerians expect from the
register?<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">&nbsp;</span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">The answers will help determine whether the
initiative becomes a dependable regional tool for transparency and fisheries
governance or simply another database whose effectiveness depends on
information that has yet to be fully verified.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">&nbsp;</span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">For Nigeria and its regional partners, the
real test is not whether a register is launched in 2027. It is whether the
information behind every entry can be trusted, acted upon and held accountable.<o:p></o:p></span></p>
