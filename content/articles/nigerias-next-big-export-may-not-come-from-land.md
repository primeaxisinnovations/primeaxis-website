<p class="MsoNormal">Nigeria has spent decades thinking about exports in terms of
what lies beneath its soil.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">Oil. Gas. Minerals. Agricultural commodities.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><o:p>&nbsp;</o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">But as the country enters a new phase of offshore
investment, another possibility is emerging.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><o:p>&nbsp;</o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">Nigeria’s next major export may not be a commodity at all.
It may be maritime capability.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><o:p>&nbsp;</o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">Offshore engineering. Fabrication. Subsea services. Ship
repair. Marine logistics. Vessel operations. Inspection. Surveying. Technical
services. The infrastructure and expertise required to support complex offshore
activity.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><o:p>&nbsp;</o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">The opportunity is becoming harder to ignore.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><o:p>&nbsp;</o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">Nigeria has introduced a new deep offshore investment
framework designed to unlock up to $50 billion in new investment, beginning
with the approximately $10 billion Bonga South West project and other large
offshore developments.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><o:p>&nbsp;</o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">The framework specifically emphasises domestic engineering,
fabrication, marine logistics, technical services and project management.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><o:p>&nbsp;</o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">The Nigerian Upstream Petroleum Regulatory Commission has
separately identified 22 major offshore projects expected between 2026 and
2030, with estimated investment potential of between $30 billion and $50
billion.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><o:p>&nbsp;</o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">Those numbers are significant for oil and gas.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><o:p>&nbsp;</o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">But they could be even more significant for Nigeria’s
maritime economy.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">THE OPPORTUNITY BENEATH THE INVESTMENT<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">Every major offshore development creates a demand ecosystem
around it.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><o:p>&nbsp;</o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">Platforms have to be engineered and maintained. Vessels have
to move people, equipment and materials. Offshore structures require
fabrication. Subsea infrastructure requires specialist engineering. Equipment
requires inspection and maintenance. Cargo moves through ports and logistics
bases. Assets must be surveyed, certified and eventually decommissioned.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><o:p>&nbsp;</o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">These are not peripheral activities.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><o:p>&nbsp;</o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">They are an industrial economy.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><o:p>&nbsp;</o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">The question Nigeria now faces is how much of that economy
will actually be built here.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><o:p>&nbsp;</o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">The Federal Government's new offshore framework recognises
the issue. It calls for projects to maximise execution within Nigeria where
commercially and technically feasible, with emphasis on domestic engineering,
fabrication, marine logistics, technical services and project management.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><o:p>&nbsp;</o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">NUPRC has now made the maritime dimension even more
explicit.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><o:p>&nbsp;</o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">At the Lagos International Maritime Week on September 8,
NUPRC Chief Executive Oritsemeyiwa Eyesan argued that offshore development must
go beyond hydrocarbon production to create a competitive ecosystem of maritime
services, infrastructure, technology and skills.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><o:p>&nbsp;</o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">The Commission identified ports, ship-repair facilities,
fabrication yards, logistics bases, storage systems and coastal transport
infrastructure as areas requiring greater investment. It also called for
stronger Nigerian participation in vessel ownership and operations, marine
logistics, subsea engineering, fabrication, equipment maintenance, inspection,
certification, environmental services and marine surveying.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><o:p>&nbsp;</o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">That is a much bigger opportunity than producing more
barrels.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">FROM SERVICING NIGERIA TO SERVING AFRICA<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">This is where the conversation becomes strategically
important.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><o:p>&nbsp;</o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">Nigeria's ports handled 35.7 million metric tons of cargo
in the second quarter of 2026, up 12.3% from the same period in 2025. Vessel
traffic also increased, with ocean-going vessel calls rising 14.4%
year-on-year.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><o:p>&nbsp;</o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">The country is expanding port infrastructure and positioning
itself as a maritime and logistics hub for West and Central Africa.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><o:p>&nbsp;</o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">But infrastructure alone does not create a maritime
industry.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><o:p>&nbsp;</o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">A port can receive a vessel without producing the company
that owns it.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><o:p>&nbsp;</o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">An offshore project can operate in Nigerian waters without
creating the Nigerian engineering firm that designs its equipment.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><o:p>&nbsp;</o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">A ship can arrive in Lagos without being repaired in
Nigeria.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><o:p>&nbsp;</o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">A fabrication contract can be executed in Nigeria while the
higher-value engineering, technology and specialised services remain elsewhere.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">That is where the real economic question lies:<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><o:p>&nbsp;</o:p></p>

<blockquote><p class="MsoNormal">Can Nigeria move from being the location of maritime
activity to becoming a producer and exporter of maritime capability?<o:p></o:p></p></blockquote>

<p class="MsoNormal"><o:p>&nbsp;</o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">The implications are significant.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><o:p>&nbsp;</o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">Instead of importing expertise to service Nigerian projects,
Nigerian companies could develop capabilities that serve projects across the
Gulf of Guinea and, eventually, wider African markets.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><o:p>&nbsp;</o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">The export would not necessarily leave Nigeria in a
container.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><o:p>&nbsp;</o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">It could leave through a Nigerian-owned vessel.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><o:p>&nbsp;</o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">A Nigerian engineering company.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">A Nigerian marine surveyor.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">A Nigerian subsea specialist.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">A Nigerian ship-repair facility.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">A Nigerian technology company providing offshore
intelligence.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><o:p>&nbsp;</o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">Or a Nigerian workforce whose expertise is in demand beyond
Nigerian waters.<o:p></o:p></p>

<h3>THE INDUSTRIAL OPPORTUNITY IS LARGER THAN OIL<o:p></o:p></h3>

<p class="MsoNormal">This is why the emerging offshore investment cycle should
not be viewed solely through the lens of production.</p>

<p class="MsoNormal">Oil and gas can become a demand engine for maritime
industrialisation.</p><p class="MsoNormal"><br></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">That distinction matters.</p>

<p class="MsoNormal">If Nigeria receives billions of dollars in offshore
investment but continues importing most of the vessels, specialised equipment,
engineering expertise and marine services required to execute those projects,
the country will increase production without necessarily building the
industrial base around it.</p>

<p class="MsoNormal">But if the same investment creates competitive Nigerian
suppliers, shipyards, logistics operators, fabrication companies, vessel
owners, engineering firms and specialised service providers, the economic
impact becomes far deeper.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><o:p>&nbsp;</o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">The objective should therefore be bigger than local
participation.</p>

<p class="MsoNormal">It should be local capability.</p>

<p class="MsoNormal">Local participation can mean being present in a contract.</p>

<p class="MsoNormal">Local capability means possessing the expertise,
infrastructure, capital and technology to compete.</p>

<p class="MsoNormal">That is the difference between content policy and industrial
development.<o:p></o:p></p>

<h2>THE EXPORT OPPORTUNITY NOBODY SHOULD OVERLOOK<o:p></o:p></h2>

<p class="MsoNormal">Nigeria does not have to limit its maritime ambitions to
servicing its own offshore projects.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">If Nigerian companies can develop world-class capabilities
around deep offshore operations, a regional market already exists beyond the
country's coastline.</p>

<p class="MsoNormal">Ghana, Angola, Equatorial Guinea, Côte d'Ivoire, Senegal, Namibia, Mozambique and other African markets require combinations of marine
logistics, engineering, vessel services, fabrication, inspection, surveying and
technical expertise.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><o:p>&nbsp;</o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">Nigeria has something particularly valuable: a large
domestic market from which these capabilities can develop and scale.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><o:p>&nbsp;</o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">The strategic opportunity is therefore straightforward.</p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><b>Build for Nigeria. Scale for Africa.</b></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">Companies that begin by servicing Nigerian offshore projects
could eventually export those services across the continent.</p>

<p class="MsoNormal">That is how a maritime economy becomes an export industry.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><o:p>&nbsp;</o:p></p>

<h3>THE TEST IS EXECUTION<o:p></o:p></h3>

<p class="MsoNormal">The danger is that Nigeria could once again mistake
investment for industrialisation.</p>

<p class="MsoNormal">Capital entering the country is not the same as capability
being created within it.</p>

<p class="MsoNormal">The $50 billion opportunity will matter. But the more
important questions will be harder to measure.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><o:p>&nbsp;</o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">How many Nigerian companies become global competitive
suppliers?</p>

<p class="MsoNormal">How many specialised vessels are Nigerian-owned and
operated?</p>

<p class="MsoNormal">How much fabrication is genuinely performed domestically?</p>

<p class="MsoNormal">How many ship-repair and maintenance facilities become
commercially viable?</p>

<p class="MsoNormal">How much offshore engineering expertise remains in Nigeria
after the projects are completed?</p>

<p class="MsoNormal">How many Nigerian professionals acquire skills that can
compete internationally?</p>

<p class="MsoNormal">And perhaps most importantly:<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">How much of the capability created for Nigeria's offshore
revival can eventually be sold to the rest of Africa?</p>

<p class="MsoNormal">Those are the metrics that will determine whether the
current offshore investment cycle becomes another extraction story or the
foundation of a broader industrial economy.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><o:p>&nbsp;</o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">PRIMEAXIS INSIGHT<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">Nigeria may be approaching an unusual economic opportunity.&nbsp;</p>

<p class="MsoNormal">The country is not simply trying to attract capital back
into its offshore sector. It is creating a potential demand pool for maritime
services, engineering, logistics and industrial capability at a scale that
could reshape the sector.&nbsp;</p>

<p class="MsoNormal">The choice is therefore bigger than oil and gas.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">Nigeria can use foreign investment primarily to produce more
hydrocarbons.</p>

<p class="MsoNormal">Or it can use that investment to build companies,
infrastructure, skills and technology capable of servicing an entire regional
maritime economy.&nbsp;</p>

<p class="MsoNormal">The first creates production.</p>

<p class="MsoNormal">The second creates an industry.&nbsp;</p>

<p class="MsoNormal">And if Nigeria gets the second right, its next major export
may not be what it takes from the ground or seabed.</p>

<p class="MsoNormal">It may be what Nigeria learns to build, operate and sell
because of them.</p>
