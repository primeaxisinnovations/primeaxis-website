Africa's maritime ambitions are increasingly defined by investments in ports, shipping, offshore energy, fisheries and subsea infrastructure. Yet behind these activities lies an asset receiving considerably less attention in the continent's economic and policy conversations: ocean data.<p>Knowledge of the seabed, water depths, underwater geological formations, ocean currents, tides, sediment movements and marine ecosystems determines how effectively countries plan, develop and protect their maritime resources.</p><p>This information is not simply a scientific resource. It is an economic and strategic asset.</p><p>For an offshore energy company, accurate seabed information can influence exploration planning, infrastructure design and operational risk. For a telecommunications operator, it informs the routing and installation of submarine cables. For port authorities, it supports dredging decisions, channel maintenance and navigational safety.</p><p>The quality of this information can therefore influence investment costs, infrastructure performance, environmental protection and maritime competitiveness.</p><p>The question facing African coastal states is whether their scientific and institutional capabilities are keeping pace with the commercial expansion of their oceans.</p><h2>THE ECONOMIC VALUE OF KNOWING THE SEABED</h2><p>Seabed mapping provides the foundation for understanding the physical characteristics of the ocean floor. Bathymetric surveys establish water depths and seabed contours, while other marine investigations examine geological structures, sediment composition and underwater environmental conditions.</p><p>Together, these datasets support decisions across the maritime economy.</p><p>The Organisation for Economic Co operation and Development, in its June 2025 policy brief, Exploring and Mapping the Ocean Depths for a More Sustainable Future, identified seabed information as fundamental to maritime management, infrastructure planning, marine conservation and the development of the wider ocean economy.</p><p>The report also highlighted a significant global information gap. At the time of its publication, approximately three quarters of the global seabed remained unmapped to modern standards.</p><p>Progress has since continued. The April 2026 release of the GEBCO global bathymetric grid reported that 28.7 per cent of the world's ocean floor had been mapped to modern standards, following the addition of almost five million square kilometres of new data.</p><p>These figures describe global coverage, not Africa's individual mapping performance. Nevertheless, they demonstrate the scale of the scientific and technological undertaking required to understand the world's oceans.</p><p>For Africa, the implications extend beyond research. Countries seeking to attract offshore investment, develop marine infrastructure and strengthen environmental oversight need reliable information about the waters within their jurisdiction.</p><h2>THE DATA GOVERNANCE QUESTION</h2><p>Collecting ocean data is only one part of the challenge. Who can access it, how it is preserved and whether it becomes available for wider national planning are equally important.</p><p>Commercial operators routinely undertake marine surveys as part of offshore exploration, infrastructure development and engineering projects. These activities can generate valuable bathymetric and geological information.</p><p>However, the information may remain within commercial databases or be subject to contractual confidentiality and security restrictions.</p><p>The OECD has identified this as a wider governance problem. Its 2025 assessment found that many national regulatory frameworks do not adequately require bathymetric information collected by private sector operators, particularly in offshore energy and telecommunications, to be shared with public authorities or international repositories.</p><p>This raises an important policy question for African governments.</p><p>When companies undertake extensive marine surveys for commercial purposes, what provisions ensure that relevant information is retained, reported and made accessible to the appropriate national institutions?</p><p>The objective should not be to undermine commercial confidentiality or compromise legitimate security interests. It should be to establish clear conditions for data preservation, national access, appropriate disclosure and institutional use.</p><p>International law already provides an important foundation.</p><p>Under the United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea, coastal states have the right to regulate and authorise marine scientific research in their territorial seas, exclusive economic zones and continental shelves, subject to the applicable provisions of the Convention.</p><p>Article 249 also provides for coastal state participation in research projects and access, upon request, to research data and samples.</p><p>&nbsp;Article 244 addresses the publication and dissemination of scientific information and the strengthening of developing countries' research capabilities.</p><p>These provisions make national research oversight and institutional capacity important components of effective ocean governance.</p><h2>NIGERIA HAS THE INSTITUTIONS. THE QUESTION IS COORDINATION.</h2><p>Nigeria already has established institutions undertaking marine research and hydrographic functions.</p><p>The Nigerian Institute for Oceanography and Marine Research conducts investigations into the physical characteristics of Nigerian waters, seabed formations, marine geology, ocean currents, sediment movements and other environmental conditions.</p><p>Its Marine Geology and Geophysics Department documents research activities covering Nigeria's coastal zone, continental shelf, continental slope and deeper marine areas. The institute also identifies collaborations with international research institutions and UNESCO's Ocean Data and Information Network for Africa.</p><p>The Office of the Surveyor General of the Federation has a hydrography department responsible for bathymetric surveys, nautical chart production, maritime geospatial information and the development of a national hydrographic data repository.</p><p>NIMASA also maintains hydrographic survey functions supporting maritime safety, including the monitoring of surveys and the provision of relevant navigational information.</p><p>These responsibilities demonstrate that Nigeria has an institutional foundation for generating and managing marine information.</p><p>However, the existence of several institutions does not automatically establish an integrated national ocean data system.</p><p>The strategic requirement is to ensure that information generated through public research, hydrographic surveys and commercial marine operations can be appropriately coordinated, preserved and used across government and industry.</p><p>It is also important to distinguish between research data, navigational information, commercially sensitive seabed surveys and environmental monitoring records. They have different purposes, access requirements and regulatory considerations.</p><p>Without clear coordination, valuable information can remain fragmented across institutions and commercial organisations, limiting its wider application.</p><h2>AFRICA'S EXISTING SCIENTIFIC NETWORK</h2><p>There are already regional efforts to address the challenge.</p><p>The Ocean Data and Information Network for Africa, supported by UNESCO's Intergovernmental Oceanographic Commission, brings together more than 40 marine related institutions across 25 African countries.</p><p>Its work focuses on improving the availability, management and application of marine and coastal information through national oceanographic data centres and regional cooperation.</p><p>This provides an existing foundation for strengthening Africa's scientific capabilities.</p><p>The next challenge is to deepen participation, improve national data management systems, strengthen technical expertise and connect research outputs more directly to the needs of governments and commercial operators.</p><p>Africa does not need to conduct every marine survey independently. International research partnerships and commercial expertise can contribute significantly to scientific development.</p><p>What matters is that cooperation strengthens national capabilities rather than leaving countries dependent on external expertise without adequate access to the resulting knowledge.</p><h2>FROM SCIENTIFIC INFORMATION TO ECONOMIC ADVANTAGE</h2><p>Ocean data should occupy a more prominent position in Africa's maritime investment and governance agenda.</p><p>Governments need to examine whether their research approval procedures, offshore licences and marine infrastructure agreements adequately address data reporting, preservation and national institutional participation.</p><p>Research institutions require sustained investment in specialised equipment, hydrographic surveying, marine geology, oceanography and data management.</p><p>Commercial operators also have a role in supporting responsible information sharing, particularly where publicly relevant information is generated through activities undertaken within national maritime jurisdictions.</p><p>The objective is not simply to collect more data.</p><p>&nbsp;It is to develop the institutional capacity to interpret it, apply it and use it to support informed economic decisions.</p><h2>PRIMEAXIS INSIGHT</h2><p>Africa's maritime competitiveness will depend not only on the resources beneath its waters but also on the quality of knowledge available to manage and develop them.</p><p>A continent seeking greater participation in offshore energy, subsea infrastructure, maritime trade and the wider blue economy cannot afford to treat ocean intelligence as a secondary scientific concern.</p><p>The ability to generate, access, preserve and apply marine information is part of the infrastructure of economic development.</p><p>For African coastal states, the next frontier of maritime capability must therefore extend beyond building ports and acquiring vessels. It must include building the scientific intelligence required to understand, govern and derive sustainable value from their oceans.</p>
