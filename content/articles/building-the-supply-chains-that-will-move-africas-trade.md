<p class="MsoNormal">Africa’s ambition to deepen intra African
trade is entering a more demanding phase. The African Continental Free Trade
Area has created the framework for a larger continental market. The harder task
is making the systems behind that market work efficiently across borders.</p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">&nbsp;</span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">Nigeria’s ports offer a current indication
of the challenge.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">In the first half of 2026, cargo throughput
at Nigerian ports increased by 12.2 percent to 68.3 million metric tonnes,
while vessel calls rose 6.9 percent to 2,300. Yet average vessel turnaround
time increased from 5.0 days in the first half of 2025 to 5.3 days. Five of the
seven ports covered in the Nigerian Ports Authority review recorded longer
turnaround times. At Lekki Port, turnaround increased from 2.1 days to 3.3
days.</span></p><p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US"><br></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">The figures reveal a tension that matters
beyond Nigeria. More cargo and more vessel activity do not automatically
produce greater efficiency.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">For a continent seeking to increase trade
within its own market, that distinction is critical.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">Dr. Obiora Madu, Chairman of Multimix Group
and a trade, logistics and supply chain expert, said Africa needs to move
beyond viewing trade primarily through national boundaries and begin organising
around continental value creation.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">&nbsp;</span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">“Agreements do not move goods; supply
chains do.”</span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">A product may be produced in one African
country, processed in another, packaged in a third and consumed in a fourth. If
each stage is treated simply as a separate national transaction, the supply
chain becomes burdened by multiple borders, procedures and regulatory systems.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">&nbsp;</span>Organising that activity as one African
value chain shifts the focus towards how efficiently production, logistics,
finance, information and markets connect across those jurisdictions.</p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">&nbsp;</span>That places greater importance on the
corridors linking African economies.</p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">&nbsp;</span>“A road is infrastructure. A corridor is an
economic system,” Madu said.</p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">For a corridor to become an economic
system, he identified connectivity, trade facilitation, productive capacity,
logistics services and market access as interconnected requirements.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">A road can connect two markets while
customs delays, poor warehousing, fragmented regulation or limited production
capacity continue to constrain the movement and value of goods.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">&nbsp;</span>Dr. Kofi Mbiah, Chief Executive Officer of
the Ghana Chamber of Shipping, has similarly highlighted trade facilitation as
a critical condition for African commerce.</p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">Mbiah has identified complex customs and
administrative procedures, inefficient and costly transit systems, informal
checkpoints and the lack of harmonised trade procedures, standards, regulations
and documentation as persistent barriers to intra African trade. He has also
emphasised efficient ports, competitive port costs, speedy cargo delivery,
intermodal connections and effective human resources as important conditions
for trade facilitation.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">Those challenges are visible in Nigeria’s
current port performance.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">The NPA data shows that cargo throughput
and vessel calls increased in the first half of 2026 while average vessel
turnaround also increased.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">&nbsp;</span>Five of the seven ports covered by the
review recorded deterioration in turnaround performance.</p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">The NPA is responsible for the
administration and management of Nigeria’s ports, including port
infrastructure, facilities, marine services and the coordination of port
operations.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">Against that responsibility, the
deterioration in vessel turnaround represents a measurable efficiency issue
within the port system.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">The increase at Lekki is particularly
significant. Average turnaround moved from 2.1 days in the first half of 2025
to 3.3 days in the first half of 2026, even as activity across the national
port system increased.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">The figures raise a straightforward
question: are existing port capacity and operations keeping pace with growing
demand?<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">Madu said infrastructure alone does not
create logistics performance. The operating architecture around the
infrastructure matters.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">Customs processes, regulatory duplication,
fragmented government agencies, poor information flows, inefficient truck
utilisation, warehousing and supply chain visibility can all influence how
effectively infrastructure performs.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">This is increasingly relevant as Nigeria
and other African economies invest in digital trade systems.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">Nigeria launched the first phase of its
National Single Window in March 2026 as part of efforts to simplify
interactions between traders and government agencies involved in cargo
clearance.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">&nbsp;</span>Madu’s assessment is that digital
transformation must begin with process transformation.</p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">“You cannot digitise what is not in
existence,” he said.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">&nbsp;</span>A fragmented process can be transferred
onto a digital platform without removing the fragmentation. The result may be
electronic bureaucracy rather than genuine trade facilitation.</p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">For digitalisation to improve African
trade, information needs to move more easily across agencies and borders,
supported by interoperable systems, common data standards, electronic
documentation, digital identities and cargo visibility.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">Mbiah’s work on African trade facilitation
has similarly emphasised coordinated systems, harmonised procedures and
efficient port processes as necessary conditions for reducing friction when
goods move across borders.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">The broader issue is the relationship
between infrastructure and economic performance.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">&nbsp;</span></p>

</div>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">&nbsp;Industry stakeholders have said significantly
greater capacity and faster cargo clearance will be necessary to support
Nigeria’s ambition of becoming a $1 trillion economy.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">&nbsp;</span>But capacity is only part of the equation.</p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">A port can have additional berths, deeper
channels and modern equipment and still underperform if vessels, cargo, trucks,
customs processes, terminal operations and government agencies are not
sufficiently coordinated.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">The same principle applies across Africa’s
emerging trade corridors.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">The physical movement of goods must connect
with production.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">Madu argues that logistics should therefore
be considered part of Africa’s industrialisation strategy rather than simply a
cost of doing business.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">Agricultural commodities illustrate the
point. A supply chain that connects farmers to aggregation, packaging, cold
chain, transportation, finance and manufacturing allows more economic activity
to remain within the continent before products reach consumers.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">The same applies to minerals and other
natural resources.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">For Africa, increasing trade volumes
without increasing the value retained from that trade would leave a significant
part of the AfCFTA opportunity unrealised.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">Mbiah has previously argued that Africa’s
regional economic blocs also need stronger integration if the continental free
trade framework is to achieve its objectives. The continent cannot operate an
effective continental market if the regional systems beneath it remain poorly
connected.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">Madu approaches the same challenge from the
supply chain perspective.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">He said Africa needs people who can connect
policy, technology, operations, finance and execution. The capability required
goes beyond managing individual logistics functions. It requires understanding
how decisions in one part of the supply chain affect production, trade,
investment and market access elsewhere.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">Taken together, the evidence points to an
interconnected system.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">AfCFTA provides the continental framework.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">&nbsp;Ports provide gateways. Roads and railways
provide connectivity. Customs and regulatory institutions determine how goods
cross jurisdictions. Digital systems connect information. Logistics providers
connect production to markets. Finance enables participation. Manufacturing
determines how much value remains within Africa.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">The performance of one component
increasingly affects the others.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">Nigeria’s port data provides a practical
example.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">&nbsp;Rising cargo volumes demonstrate growing
demand, while longer vessel turnaround times show that increased activity does
not automatically translate into increased efficiency.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">For Nigeria’s port system, the issue is
measurable. More cargo is moving through the ports, but vessels are taking
longer to turn around at most of the ports covered by the NPA review.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">Across Africa, the same operational
challenge extends to the corridors that will carry AfCFTA trade.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">The continental market is being built. The
supply chain architecture required to make that market work is still being
developed.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<h2><span lang="EN-US">PRIMEAXIS INSIGHT<o:p></o:p></span></h2>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">Africa’s trade integration will
increasingly be measured by what happens after agreements are signed.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">The decisive indicators will be how quickly
goods cross borders, how efficiently ports and corridors handle growing
volumes, how effectively digital systems remove friction, and how much
production and value remain within the continent.<o:p></o:p></span></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><span lang="EN-US">AfCFTA provides the framework. The
performance of Africa’s supply chains will determine how much of its economic
potential becomes reality.<o:p></o:p></span></p>
