<p>West and Central Africa's ambition to expand coastal ferry transportation is exposing a significant gap between the region's demand for alternative transport systems and its capacity to deliver the infrastructure, investment and regulatory coordination required to sustain them.</p><p>The proposed development of cross border ferry services along the Dakar to Luanda coastal corridor presents an opportunity to improve regional trade, reduce pressure on road transportation and create additional channels for moving passengers and cargo. However, turning this ambition into a functioning maritime network will require considerably more than the deployment of vessels.</p><p>This was a central issue at the inaugural West and Central Africa Ferry Conference and Exhibition 2026, held at the Civic Centre, Victoria Island, Lagos, where stakeholders called for increased investment, stronger regulatory frameworks and improved maritime infrastructure to support regional connectivity.</p><p>Dr Edmund Chilaka, Visiting Research Fellow at Liverpool John Moores University and Adjunct Lecturer at the University of Lagos, highlighted the growing demand for alternative transportation systems, citing reports that more than 500 articulated trucks queue daily at the Badagry Seme border.</p><p>The congestion illustrates the logistical pressures facing businesses moving goods across neighbouring countries. Multiple checkpoints, prolonged border delays and the associated costs of road transportation continue to constrain regional commerce.</p><p>Chilaka argued that coastal ferry services could provide a safer and potentially more cost effective alternative for passengers and cargo. He also pointed to concerns reportedly expressed by manufacturers and traders, including Innoson Motors and Dangote Group, about the difficulties of transporting goods to other West and Central African markets.</p><p>His assessment was that the demand for coastal ferry operations already exists, but the region lacks sufficient vessels to meet the market's requirements.</p><p>“The market is ready, there are cargos to be shipped, but there are no ferries,” he stated.</p><p>He urged existing operators to expand their services and called on governments and regulatory agencies, particularly the Nigerian Maritime Administration and Safety Agency (NIMASA), to establish conditions that encourage investment and support emerging operators.</p><p>Yet vessel availability represents only one part of the challenge. Ferry operations depend on navigable waterways, functional terminals, reliable security arrangements, appropriate vessel standards and predictable regulatory conditions. Without these foundations, additional vessels may not translate into sustainable services.</p><h2>THE INFRASTRUCTURE REQUIREMENT</h2><p>The importance of infrastructure and operational readiness was emphasised by Oluwadamilola Emmanuel, Special Adviser to the Lagos State Governor on Blue Economy and General Manager of the Lagos State Waterways Authority (LASWA).</p><p>He cautioned that deploying vessels without properly dredged waterways, effective security arrangements and functional supporting facilities could compromise safety and long term sustainability.</p><p>Emmanuel highlighted Lagos State's efforts to strengthen waterways management through technology, infrastructure development and collaboration. He disclosed that the state had established a water wave monitoring and data management centre, with artificial intelligence supporting its control room and predictive models for mapping proposed trips.</p><p>He also identified the introduction of West Africa's first electric ferry and the ongoing €410 million Omi Eko Integrated Mass Transit Project as significant developments in the state's water transportation programme.</p><p>The project is expected to cover approximately 15 ferry routes spanning 140 kilometres, upgrade or construct 25 terminals and jetties, and introduce 78 electric powered vessels.</p><p>These investments illustrate the scale of planning required to develop a structured ferry network, even within a single state. Extending connectivity across national boundaries introduces additional requirements involving infrastructure compatibility, operating standards, safety oversight and cross border regulatory arrangements.</p><p>Emmanuel stressed that no single state could independently deliver the level of connectivity required across the region, making cooperation among neighbouring states and countries essential.</p><h2>THE REGULATORY AND COMMERCIAL CHALLENGE</h2><p>The proposed Dakar to Luanda corridor is significant because of its potential to connect coastal markets and support the movement of goods across West and Central Africa.</p><p>The African Continental Free Trade Area provides access to a market of approximately 1.3 billion people. However, the existence of a large continental market does not automatically produce efficient trade. Businesses need transportation systems capable of moving goods reliably, affordably and across borders without introducing additional delays.</p><p>For regional ferry services, this requires coordination among maritime regulators, port authorities, customs administrations and security agencies.</p><p>Vessel certification, passenger documentation, cargo handling, port procedures and safety requirements must be addressed through workable arrangements between participating countries. Otherwise, the administrative difficulties experienced by road transport operators could reappear in maritime operations.</p><p>The commercial question is equally important.</p><p>Professor David Aworawo of the Department of History and Strategic Studies, University of Lagos, who chaired the conference, described coastal ferry transportation as an opportunity to accelerate economic development and facilitate the movement of people and goods.</p><p>He emphasised the importance of understanding route economics, the business environment and operational requirements to ensure that ferry services remain commercially viable.</p><p>This consideration should shape the development of any proposed regional network. Passenger demand, cargo volumes, vessel capacity, journey duration, operating costs, terminal charges and turnaround times will determine whether individual routes can sustain commercial operations.</p><p>Delivering the keynote address, Capt. Ade Olopoenia also drew attention to the difficulties facing travellers and small scale traders, including poor road conditions, extortion and prolonged border delays. He argued that the limited availability of safe and reliable maritime alternatives continues to restrict regional mobility.</p><p>His observations reinforce the case for expanding transportation choices, but the success of coastal ferry services will ultimately depend on whether operators can offer dependable alternatives that businesses and passengers are willing to use.</p><h2>FROM REGIONAL AMBITION TO IMPLEMENTATION</h2><p>For Nigeria, the development of regional ferry connectivity presents an opportunity to strengthen its position in West and Central African trade and maritime transportation.</p><p>However, translating this opportunity into commercial operations will require clearly defined responsibilities among government institutions, regulators and private investors.</p><p>NIMASA's role in establishing an enabling regulatory environment will be important, alongside cooperation with port authorities, customs administrations, security agencies and their counterparts across participating countries.</p><p>Governments must also address the financing of supporting infrastructure, the commercial risks facing operators and the arrangements required to coordinate services across national boundaries.</p><p>The next phase therefore needs to move beyond broad commitments towards route feasibility assessments, investment structures, infrastructure priorities and clearly established regulatory responsibilities.</p><p>Without these foundations, the proposed coastal ferry network risks remaining an attractive regional proposition without the operational capacity to deliver its promised economic benefits.</p><h2>PRIMEAXIS INSIGHT</h2><p>West and Central Africa's coastal ferry ambition is fundamentally an infrastructure, investment and governance question. The region has a substantial coastline, established commercial relationships and a growing need for more efficient transportation options. What remains unresolved is how these advantages will be translated into a coordinated and commercially sustainable maritime network.</p><p>Lagos State's Omi Eko project demonstrates the scale of investment and institutional preparation required to develop structured ferry services. Regional connectivity, however, demands an additional layer of cooperation involving multiple governments, regulators and private operators.</p><p>For Nigeria, the opportunity extends beyond providing ferry services. It includes developing competitive maritime operators, supporting regional trade and strengthening the country's participation in the transportation networks that will shape African commerce.</p><p>The real measure of progress will not be the number of routes proposed or vessels announced. It will be the establishment of operational connections, measurable growth in passenger and cargo movements, improved journey reliability and commercially sustainable services.</p><p>The region has identified the opportunity. The critical test now is whether its governments, regulators and investors can deliver the system required to realise it.</p>
