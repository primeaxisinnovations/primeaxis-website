<p>Nigeria’s renewed enforcement of its Cabotage regime raises a question that goes beyond vessel inspections and regulatory compliance. After more than two decades of seeking to strengthen indigenous shipping, is Nigeria building the commercial capacity to carry more of its own cargo, or is enforcement advancing faster than the industry it is intended to support?</p><p><br></p><p>The distinction is critical. Inspections can identify noncompliance. Detentions can reinforce the consequences of violating applicable requirements. Neither, by itself, establishes that Nigerian shipping companies are securing contracts, operating vessels profitably or capturing a greater share of the value generated by maritime transport.</p><p><br></p><p>On 21 September 2026, the Nigerian Maritime Administration and Safety Agency (NIMASA) announced Operation Blue Green, an intelligence led enforcement initiative. Subsequent enforcement under Operation Sea Guardian brought Cabotage compliance back into industry discussions.</p><p><br></p><p>Following initial reports about vessels allegedly detained, NIMASA clarified that only MT Holmes and MT Sabeak were detained for noncompliance. MT Fujairah Energy, JS Gratitude and MT Bora were compliant and were not detained.</p><p>The distinction matters for accurate reporting and confidence in regulatory enforcement. But the larger question remains: what should Nigeria expect enforcement to deliver for its indigenous shipping industry?</p><p><br></p><p>The Coastal and Inland Shipping (Cabotage) Act, enacted in 2003, was designed to promote indigenous participation in domestic shipping.</p><p><br></p><p>&nbsp;Its wider economic purpose is to help Nigerian operators develop the capacity to serve a market that supports trade, energy, offshore activity and industrial production.</p><p><br></p><p>That objective requires more than enforcing the rules. It requires a commercially viable shipping industry capable of responding to the opportunities that enforcement is intended to protect.</p><h2>THE DIFFERENCE BETWEEN OWNING SHIPS AND BUILDING CAPACITY</h2><p>In PrimeAxis Executive Conversation, published on 14 October 2026, Oluseyi Oluyede, General Manager of Maritime Shipping and Ocean International Resources Limited (MSOIR), explained why vessel ownership alone is not a sufficient measure of industry development.</p><p><br></p><p>“Capacity is not just about the number of vessels or putting ships on the water. It is the ability to win a charter, operate a ship safely, repay loans on time and repeat the process. If you own a vessel but cannot run it commercially, there is no value in that ownership.”</p><p><br></p><p>Her argument places commercial performance at the centre of indigenous shipping development. A vessel is an asset, but its value depends on its ability to secure employment, operate safely and generate sufficient revenue to sustain the business.</p><p><br></p><p>Oluyede also stressed the importance of technical and managerial competence.</p><p><br></p><p>“Competence matters more than tonnage. We need competent people in chartering, ship management, voyage estimation and protection and indemnity insurance. These are areas where the industry needs more capacity. We have to build our people, our ships and our management capabilities together.”</p><p><br></p><p>The implication is that fleet expansion without the corresponding development of expertise could leave Nigerian operators with expensive assets but insufficient capacity to operate them profitably.</p><p><br></p><p>She recommends beginning with areas where Nigeria has practical opportunities.</p><p><br></p><p>“We should start where Nigeria has opportunities, such as coastal tankers, offshore support vessels, barges, short sea shipping and bulk shipping close to the cargo. We can use the Cabotage regime and progressively build the capacity to operate in international waters.”</p><p><br></p><p>This approach prioritises a gradual expansion built on demonstrable performance. Successful contracts can establish a track record, strengthen lenders’ confidence and support further investment.</p><p><br></p><p>“We need to build a track record and evaluate our initiatives. A contract performed successfully and on time becomes the basis for securing another contract and financing another vessel.”</p><p><br></p><p>For Nigeria, the challenge is therefore not simply to increase the number of indigenous vessels. It is to develop companies that can secure contracts, fulfil them reliably and reinvest in sustainable growth.</p><h2>CARGO MUST COME BEFORE TONNAGE</h2><p>In PrimeAxis Executive Conversation, published on 25 September 2026, Alhaji Aminu Umar, President of the Nigerian Chamber of Shipping and Managing Director of Sea Transport Services Nigeria Limited, identified access to capital as a major barrier to indigenous shipping.</p><p><br></p><p>“One of our biggest challenges in acquiring those assets is access to capital.”</p><p>Shipping requires substantial investment, while lenders need confidence that operators can repay their loans. Umar explained what financiers want to see when considering an application.</p><p><br></p><p>“What all financiers want to see as part of the document to be able to give you access to capital is that you have a contract that covers the time he will completely collect his funds back.”</p><p><br></p><p>His point is that access to finance cannot be separated from the availability of dependable cargo. Long term contracts with major cargo owners can provide revenue visibility and improve the prospects of financing vessels.</p><p><br></p><p>This also explains why acquiring ships before establishing demand can expose operators to considerable commercial risk.</p><p>Oluyede described the approach adopted by MSOIR:</p><p>“Our model is not to buy ships and then hope to find cargo. It is to secure cargo and build tonnage around proven demand.”</p><p><br></p><p>The principle has wider implications for government policy and the relationship between cargo owners, shipowners and financiers. Where government controlled cargo can lawfully and practically support qualified indigenous operators, it may provide an anchor for developing domestic capacity. Major private cargo owners can also help by offering contracts that allow capable operators to plan investment around credible demand.</p><p><br></p><p>The objective should be to connect cargo opportunities with operators who can meet the required commercial, technical and safety standards.</p><p><br></p><p>Umar also called for greater involvement by financial institutions in shipping finance, including specialist products suited to the industry and consideration of capital market funding. These measures could widen the available financing options, although their effectiveness would depend on commercially viable projects, suitable contract structures and the cost of capital.</p><p><br></p><p>For indigenous shipping to grow sustainably, cargo commitments, financing and vessel acquisition must be treated as connected decisions rather than separate interventions.</p><h2>ENFORCEMENT IS ONLY ONE PART OF THE FRAMEWORK</h2><p>Umar’s position on Cabotage implementation is direct. He emphasised the need to enforce the law in support of indigenous shipowners.</p><p><br></p><p>“The Cabotage law has to be implemented.”</p><p>He also summarised its purpose in three words:</p><p>“In short, the Cabotage law says PROMOTE, PROTECT AND PROVIDE for local shipping companies.”</p><p><br></p><p>The effectiveness of that objective, however, depends on the environment in which Nigerian operators are expected to compete.</p><p><br></p><p>Oluyede called for policy consistency, transparency in the administration of the Cabotage Vessel Financing Fund (CVFF), improved access to long tenure financing and a reduction in operating costs. She identified port and clearance costs, turnaround times, cargo dwell time, demurrage and illegal charges as factors affecting operators’ competitiveness.</p><p><br></p><p>She also cautioned against government becoming a shipowner and operator.</p><p><br></p><p>“Government should not own and operate ships. We should learn from the experience of the Nigerian National Shipping Line, which operated from 1959 to 1995. Government should be an enabler, not the owner of the business.”</p><p><br></p><p>Her position is that public intervention should reduce barriers and support viable private operators without replacing the commercial discipline required to run a shipping business.</p><p><br></p><p>The industry also needs better data to guide decisions. Umar highlighted the absence of consolidated long term maritime cargo and vessel data, including information on seasonal demand and cargo patterns. Such information could help operators identify viable routes, cargo owners plan transport requirements and lenders assess investment opportunities.</p><p><br></p><p>Oluyede similarly advocated measuring the performance of interventions rather than relying on announcements or approvals. Relevant indicators include equity invested, debt approved and actually disbursed, vessel utilisation, debt service capacity and contract duration.</p><p><br></p><p>These measures would offer a clearer picture of whether Cabotage implementation is producing commercially sustainable indigenous capacity.</p><h2>PRIMEAXIS INSIGHT</h2><p>Nigeria’s Cabotage challenge is fundamentally economic. Enforcement can establish the consequences of noncompliance, but building an indigenous shipping industry requires the alignment of regulation, cargo, finance, operational competence and supporting infrastructure.</p><p><br></p><p>Oluyede’s analysis shows why vessel ownership must lead to productive commercial activity.</p><p><br></p><p>&nbsp;Umar’s comments demonstrate why financing depends on credible contracts and the ability to generate revenue over time. Together, their perspectives point to an industry development strategy grounded in market demand rather than vessel acquisition alone.</p><p><br></p><p>There is also a wider economic opportunity. Oluyede explained the significance of retaining shipping expenditure within Nigeria:</p><p>“When freight and insurance payments go abroad, we lose foreign exchange. Nigerian owned tonnage carrying Nigerian cargo is a form of import substitution for services. We must build the capacity to retain more of that value within the country.”</p><p><br></p><p>Developing indigenous shipping could therefore support more than shipowners. It could create demand for technical services, ship repair, marine engineering, skilled labour and related businesses. However, those benefits depend on building capabilities that meet commercial and international standards.</p><p><br></p><p>Oluyede outlined the sequence she considers necessary for sustainable development:</p><p>“The sequence should be cargo assurance, then contracts, ship repair and shipbuilding.”</p><p>This is a practical framework for assessing future policy. Cargo opportunities must be identified, qualified operators must be able to secure contracts, financing must support viable investments, and technical capabilities must develop alongside the fleet.</p><p><br></p><p>NIMASA’s enforcement operations provide an opportunity to assess how the Cabotage framework is being implemented. They do not, on their own, establish that indigenous shipping capacity has grown.</p><p><br></p><p>The next measure of progress should be whether more Nigerian operators can secure and fulfil viable contracts, keep vessels productively employed, develop technical expertise and retain a greater share of shipping related value within the economy.</p><p><br></p><p>The real test of Cabotage is not simply whether Nigeria can enforce the rules. It is whether Nigerian shipping businesses are becoming capable of competing under them.</p>
