<p class="MsoNormal">Offshore energy, shipping, ports, marine transport,
fisheries, coastal tourism, marine construction and other parts of the blue
economy are creating demand for increasingly specialised skills.<br>
The usual response is to ask what Nigeria needs to acquire.<o:p></o:p></p>

<h2>But there is another question worth asking:<o:p></o:p></h2>

<p class="MsoNormal">What if part of the capability Nigeria needs is already
present along its coastline, but has never been systematically developed?<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">Across Nigeria’s coastal communities are people whose lives
have given them an intimate understanding of the water.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">Boat operators understand local waterways, channels, tides
and currents through years of experience. Fishermen develop practical knowledge
of weather, water movement and seasonal conditions. Young people grow up around
boats and waterways, developing levels of water confidence that others must
acquire later.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">None of this makes them professional mariners,
hydrographers, commercial divers or marine engineers.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">But it may provide a starting point for identifying
aptitude.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">The opportunity is therefore not to romanticise informal
knowledge or substitute it for professional standards.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">It is to build a system capable of converting existing
knowledge, aptitude and practical experience into recognised maritime
capability.<o:p></o:p></p>

<h2>THE GAP BETWEEN KNOWLEDGE AND CAPABILITY<o:p></o:p></h2>

<p class="MsoNormal">Consider a coastal boat operator who has spent years
navigating local waterways but has never received formal training in
navigation.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">Experience does not make that person a qualified navigator.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">However, familiarity with the operating environment may
provide a foundation upon which formal education, technical training,
assessment and supervised experience can build.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">The same principle applies to water based skills.<br>
A strong swimmer is not a commercial diver.<br>
Commercial diving requires specialised training, medical assessment, equipment,
procedures and certification.<br>
A person who understands local coastal conditions is not automatically a
hydrographer.<br>
Hydrography requires technical knowledge, equipment, data collection and
professional competence.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">This distinction is critical.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">The opportunity is not to replace professional
qualifications with indigenous knowledge.<br>
It is to recognise where existing knowledge and aptitude can become the
starting point for professional development.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">Knowledge is not capability. But knowledge can be the
beginning of capability.<o:p></o:p></p>

<h2>THE CONVERSION CHAIN<o:p></o:p></h2>

<p class="MsoNormal">A serious maritime talent strategy must connect a sequence
that is often treated as separate:<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><b><i>Identification</i></b> → <b><i>Assessment</i></b> → <b><i>Education</i></b>
→ <b><i>Technical Training</i></b> → <b><i>Certification</i></b> → <b><i>Industry
Experience</i></b> → <b><i>Employment or Enterprise</i></b> → <b><i>Exportable
Capability</i></b><o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">The first step is finding potential.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">The second is determining what that potential can
realistically become.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">Training provides the technical foundation.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">Certification establishes professional recognition.
Practical experience converts training into competence. Employment or
enterprise creates economic value.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">Only after these stages does capability become genuinely
exportable.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">This is the difference between having talent and building
capability.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">Nigeria already has elements of this system through maritime
education, cadetship programmes, technical training, certification and industry
linked human capital initiatives.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">The more important question is whether these interventions
can become part of a deeper and more connected national capability pipeline.<o:p></o:p></p>

<h2>THE BLUE ECONOMY NEEDS MORE THAN INFRASTRUCTURE<o:p></o:p></h2>

<p class="MsoNormal">The blue economy will require far more than ships, ports and
offshore infrastructure.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">It will require marine engineers, navigators, hydrographers,
survey technicians, divers, vessel operators, marine electricians, naval
architects, environmental specialists, port and terminal professionals, safety
specialists, marine construction workers, logistics professionals and other
technical occupations.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">Not all of these careers require the same educational route.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">Some require university degrees.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">Others require technical and vocational training, practical
experience and recognised certification.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">That diversity should be treated as an advantage.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">A coastal talent strategy should not assume that every
candidate needs a university degree before entering the maritime economy.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">The objective should be to identify aptitude and match
people to appropriate pathways.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">But that requires visibility. <br>
A young person living in a coastal community cannot pursue a career path they
do not know exists.<br>
A training institution cannot develop talent it never reaches.<br>
An employer cannot absorb capability that the training system does not prepare.<br>
This is where the talent pipeline becomes an economic question.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><o:p>&nbsp;</o:p></p>

<h2>TRAINING MUST CONNECT TO DEMAND<o:p></o:p></h2>

<p class="MsoNormal">Training people is not the same as building capability.<br>
The real test is what happens after training.<br>
<b><i>Can the person obtain certification?<br>
Can they gain practical experience?<br>
Can an employer recognise their competence?<br>
Can they enter an industry where their skills are needed?<br>
Can they eventually build a business around that expertise?</i></b><o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">This is why maritime human capital development must be
demand led.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">Training should be connected to identifiable opportunities
in shipping, offshore operations, ports, marine construction, surveying,
environmental services, logistics and other parts of the maritime economy.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">Nigeria’s existing human capital programmes provide examples
of this approach, particularly where industry partners are involved in
training, practical exposure and pathways towards employment. The lesson is
broader than any individual programme.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">The strongest talent pipeline is one that begins with
industry demand and works backwards to identify, train and prepare the people
capable of meeting it.<o:p></o:p></p>

<h2>FROM LOCAL PARTICIPATION TO LOCAL CAPABILITY<o:p></o:p></h2>

<p class="MsoNormal">This takes the local content conversation a step further.<br>
Local participation asks whether Nigerians are present in maritime projects.<br>
Local capability asks whether Nigerians possess the expertise required to
perform, manage and eventually originate the work themselves. The difference is
significant.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">Participation can create employment around a project.<br>
Capability can create professionals, companies, intellectual property,
specialised services and recurring economic value.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">A Nigerian professional who develops internationally
recognised expertise can deploy that capability across projects and markets.<br>
A Nigerian company that develops a specialised marine service can build on that
expertise repeatedly.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">The progression is therefore:<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><b><i>Participation</i></b> → <b><i>Capability</i></b> → <b><i>Ownership</i></b>
→ <b><i>Enterprise</i></b> → <b><i>Export</i></b><o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><o:p>&nbsp;</o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">That is where human capital becomes an economic asset.<o:p></o:p></p>

<h2>THE AFRICAN MARKET BEYOND NIGERIA<o:p></o:p></h2>

<p class="MsoNormal">Nigeria’s maritime opportunity does not stop at its
territorial waters.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">Across Africa, ports are expanding, offshore activity is
evolving, marine infrastructure is developing and the wider blue economy is
attracting investment.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">This creates a larger question.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">Can Nigeria develop a maritime workforce that serves
domestic demand alone, or can it develop expertise capable of competing across
regional markets?<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">Those are different propositions. Exportable capability
cannot be built simply by training more people.<br>
It requires standards that employers and clients can trust. <br>
It requires recognised certification.<br>
It requires practical experience.<br>
It requires professional discipline.<br>
It requires institutions capable of producing people whose competence is
understood beyond Nigeria.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">The objective should not be to export people simply because
they are Nigerian.<br>
It should be to develop expertise that is valuable because it meets recognised
professional standards.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">That is a fundamentally different proposition.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><o:p>&nbsp;</o:p></p>

<h2>THE SYSTEM BETWEEN TALENT AND OPPORTUNITY<o:p></o:p></h2>

<p class="MsoNormal">Nigeria does not lack maritime institutions.<br>
It does not lack coastal communities.<br>
It does not lack young people seeking economic opportunity.<br>
It does not lack examples of human capital programmes.<br>
What remains underdeveloped is the bridge connecting these assets at scale.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">A young person in a coastal community may possess aptitude
but have little knowledge of maritime career pathways.<br>
A training institution may offer a programme but have limited reach into the
communities where potential candidates can be identified.<br>
An employer may need specialised workers but struggle to find people with the
right combination of technical training, certification and practical
experience.<br>
An industry programme may successfully train a cohort without creating a
mechanism for replicating the model across other communities and sectors. This
is the capability gap. Not necessarily a shortage of people.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">A shortage of conversion systems.<br>
Nigeria needs a more deliberate architecture connecting:<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">Coastal communities<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">&nbsp;<b><i>→ Talent
identification<o:p></o:p></i></b></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><b><i>&nbsp;→ Aptitude
assessment<o:p></o:p></i></b></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><b><i>&nbsp;→ Appropriate
education<o:p></o:p></i></b></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><b><i>&nbsp;→ Technical
training<o:p></o:p></i></b></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><b><i>&nbsp;→ Certification<o:p></o:p></i></b></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><b><i>&nbsp;→ Sea time or
industry exposure<o:p></o:p></i></b></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><b><i>&nbsp;→ Employment
and enterprise<o:p></o:p></i></b></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><b><i>&nbsp;→ Continuous
professional development<o:p></o:p></i></b></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><b><i>&nbsp;→ Regional and
international deployment<o:p></o:p></i></b></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">That is how a workforce becomes a capability base.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><o:p>&nbsp;</o:p></p>

<h2>PRIMEAXIS INSIGHT<o:p></o:p></h2>

<p class="MsoNormal">Nigeria has spent considerable attention asking what its
maritime economy needs.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">More ships.<br>
More infrastructure.<br>
More technology.<br>
More investment.<br>
More specialised expertise.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">Those needs are real.<br>
But there is another strategic asset much closer to home.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><b><i>People</i></b>.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">Not simply people looking for jobs.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">People carrying knowledge, aptitude, practical experience
and familiarity with the marine environment that can potentially be developed
into professional capability.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">The coastal boat operator is not automatically a marine
professional.<br>
The young swimmer is not automatically a commercial diver.<br>
The fisherman with generations of practical knowledge is not automatically a
hydrographer.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">But neither should their existing abilities be dismissed
simply because they do not yet carry the qualifications of the formal maritime
economy.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">The strategic opportunity lies in the conversion.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">Knowledge can become skill.<br>
Skill can become competence. <br>
Competence can become certification.<br>
Certification can become professional capability.<br>
Professional capability can become enterprise.<br>
Enterprise can become export.<br>
Nigeria already has pieces of this chain.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">The next question is whether those pieces can be connected
more deliberately to the people and communities that sit furthest from the
formal maritime economy.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">That is where the blue economy conversation needs to become
more sophisticated.<br>
It is not only about attracting capital.<br>
It is about developing the human capability required to absorb, execute and
retain that capital.<br>
It is not only about increasing Nigerian participation.<br>
It is about developing Nigerians who can perform specialised work to
internationally recognised standards.<br>
It is not only about creating jobs.<br>
It is about creating expertise that can generate companies, services,
intellectual property and export revenue.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">The opportunity is bigger than recruitment.<br>
It is capability formation.<br>
If Nigeria can build the systems that connect coastal communities to education,
technical training, certification and industry demand, it can begin converting
an underdeveloped pool of indigenous knowledge and natural aptitude into a
recognised maritime capability base.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><o:p>&nbsp;</o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">And that could change the meaning of local content itself. The
objective should no longer be simply to ensure that Nigerians participate in
maritime projects.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">It should be to ensure that Nigeria develops people and
companies capable of originating, executing, owning and eventually exporting
maritime expertise. <br>
The question is no longer simply whether Nigeria has maritime talent.<br>
It is whether Nigeria has built the system capable of finding it, developing it
and turning it into economic value.<br>
That is where the next phase of Nigeria’s maritime capability must begin.<o:p></o:p></p>
