<h1><span style="color: rgb(13, 31, 40); font-family: Montserrat, &quot;Montserrat Fallback&quot;, system-ui, sans-serif; font-size: 15px; font-weight: normal;">Nigeria has been politically independent for 66 years.</span></h1><p>At sea, the more difficult question is economic.</p><p>Who owns the ships carrying Nigeria's cargo?</p><p>Who finances them?</p><p>Who earns the freight?</p><p>Who provides the specialised capacity behind the country's coastal, offshore and international trade?</p><p>And, ultimately, how much of the economic value created by Nigeria's maritime activity stays in Nigeria?</p><p>That is the question worth asking this Independence Day.</p><p>Because Nigeria is not short of maritime assets.</p><p>It has an 853 kilometre coastline, extensive inland waterways, major ports, a large domestic market and one of Africa's biggest trade economies.</p><p>The country generates the cargo.</p><p>The harder challenge is capturing more of the business that cargo creates.</p><h2>THE CARGO IS NIGERIAN. WHERE DOES THE VALUE GO?</h2><p>Nigeria's freight market is substantial.</p><p>BusinessDay reported in March that the country's freight market was worth more than $6 billion annually and that industry sources estimated more than 80 percent of freight earnings were going to foreign players. The report also cited the long decline of indigenous shipping capacity following the collapse of the former national shipping line.</p><p>That figure is an industry estimate, not an official national account.</p><p>But it points to a question that is difficult to dismiss.</p><p>If Nigeria generates the cargo, why does so much of the commercial value associated with moving that cargo remain outside Nigerian ownership?</p><p>This is where the maritime conversation needs to move.</p><p>For years, Nigeria's maritime development debate has concentrated on infrastructure.</p><p>Build better ports.</p><p>Dredge channels.</p><p>Improve cargo clearance.</p><p>Digitise processes.</p><p>Strengthen security.</p><p>Modernise terminals.</p><p>All of these are necessary.</p><p>But infrastructure efficiency and economic ownership are not the same thing.</p><p>A more efficient port can process more cargo without creating a Nigerian owned fleet.</p><p>A digital clearance platform can reduce delays without creating Nigerian maritime capital.</p><p>A deeper channel can accommodate larger vessels without putting those vessels under Nigerian ownership.</p><p>Infrastructure creates the platform.</p><p>Ownership determines who captures more of the value.</p><p>That distinction is becoming increasingly important.</p><h2>NIGERIA IS NOW INVESTING IN THE SECTOR. BUT WHAT IS IT BUILDING?</h2><p>There has been a significant shift in the policy architecture around Nigeria's maritime economy.</p><p>The Federal Ministry of Marine and Blue Economy was established in 2023.</p><p>The country adopted its first National Policy on Marine and Blue Economy in 2025.</p><p>Port infrastructure is receiving renewed attention beyond Lagos, including the approved modernisation of Onne, Rivers, Delta and Calabar.</p><p>And the sector's public revenue has risen sharply.</p><p>According to the Ministry's three year scorecard, agencies under the ministry generated ₦1.83 trillion in 2025, compared with ₦700.79 billion in 2023, an increase of 160 percent.</p><p>That is significant.</p><p>But it raises a bigger question.</p><p>If maritime activity can generate this level of public revenue, how much private Nigerian wealth is the sector generating through ownership?</p><p>Those are different measurements.</p><p>Government revenue can rise.</p><p>Port throughput can rise.</p><p>Cargo volumes can rise.</p><p>And yet the underlying ownership structure can remain weak.</p><p>That is the gap Nigeria now has to confront.</p><h2>THE CABOTAGE PROMISE</h2><p>Nigeria has actually had a policy instrument for indigenous maritime ownership for more than two decades.</p><p>The Coastal and Inland Shipping, or Cabotage, Act of 2003 was designed to reserve domestic coastal and inland shipping to Nigerian owned and Nigerian flagged vessels, subject to the law's requirements and waiver provisions.</p><p>NIMASA's own description of the regime is explicit: Cabotage is intended to encourage indigenous shipping, while the CVFF was established to provide financial assistance for vessel acquisition.</p><p>The law also requires vessels participating in the regime to meet Nigerian ownership, registration, manning and construction requirements.</p><p>Where indigenous capacity is unavailable, however, waivers can be processed. NIMASA's published guidelines specifically provide for waivers where, among other things, there is no Nigerian shipbuilding company capable of constructing the vessel required.</p><p>And here lies the structural problem.</p><p>The law sought to create indigenous capacity, but the market often needed capacity before indigenous operators had been able to build it.</p><p>The waiver therefore became a practical bridge between policy ambition and market reality.</p><p>But a bridge that remains in place for too long can become part of the structure.</p><h2>THE CVFF IS THE REAL TEST</h2><p>This is why the Cabotage Vessel Financing Fund matters so much.</p><p>The CVFF was not created simply to provide another government intervention.</p><p>Its stated objective is to help Nigerian operators acquire vessels and increase indigenous ship ownership. It is funded in part by a 2 percent surcharge on contracts performed by vessels engaged in Cabotage trade.</p><p>The logic is straightforward.</p><p>Nigeria cannot build indigenous shipping capacity without vessels.</p><p>Vessels require capital.</p><p>Capital requires bankable projects.</p><p>Bankable projects require predictable revenue.</p><p>Predictable revenue requires cargo.</p><p>And cargo must ultimately be available to Nigerian operators at commercially viable terms.</p><p>That means the CVFF alone cannot solve the ownership problem.</p><p>It can provide capital.</p><p>It cannot create cargo.</p><p>It cannot build a shipyard.</p><p>It cannot guarantee a charter contract.</p><p>It cannot train every engineer required.</p><p>It cannot make a vessel commercially competitive.</p><p>That is why the current phase of CVFF implementation deserves to be watched not as a disbursement exercise, but as an industrial policy test.</p><p>How many productive Nigerian owned vessels will the fund ultimately create?</p><p>That is the number that matters.</p><h2>MONEY IS ONLY ONE HALF OF SHIP FINANCE</h2><p>This is where Nigeria's maritime finance problem becomes more complicated.</p><p>A bank does not finance a ship merely because the government wants more Nigerian vessels.</p><p>The bank needs to understand how the asset will earn.</p><p>Who will charter it?</p><p>For how long?</p><p>At what rate?</p><p>What cargo will it carry?</p><p>Who is the counterparty?</p><p>What happens if the contract ends?</p><p>How will maintenance be funded?</p><p>What is the resale value?</p><p>What insurance and classification arrangements are in place?</p><p>The vessel therefore needs more than financing.</p><p>It needs a business model.</p><p>That is why the conversation around indigenous shipping increasingly has to include Nigeria's major cargo owners.</p><p>Long term cargo contracts can provide the revenue visibility required to support vessel acquisition.</p><p>Without cargo commitments, even concessional maritime finance can struggle to translate into sustainable fleet growth.</p><p>This is the part of the conversation that is often missing.</p><p>Nigeria asks:</p><p>Why are Nigerian shipowners not buying more ships?</p><p>The next question should be:</p><p>What commercial structure would make those ships bankable?</p><h2>THE SHIPYARD PROBLEM</h2><p>There is another reason ownership cannot be solved by finance alone.</p><p>A fleet requires an industrial ecosystem.</p><p>Ships need maintenance.</p><p>They need dry docking.</p><p>They need classification.</p><p>They need marine engineering.</p><p>They need spare parts.</p><p>They need fabrication.</p><p>They need trained crews.</p><p>They need specialist insurance.</p><p>They need technical management.</p><p>This is why Nigeria's ship repair and shipbuilding capacity matters as much as vessel acquisition.</p><p>The country can finance a fleet and still remain dependent on foreign facilities for significant parts of the lifecycle of those vessels.</p><p>That would improve ownership without fully solving maritime industrial dependence.</p><p>The ambition therefore has to be bigger than buying ships.</p><p>It has to be building the ecosystem around them.</p><h2>THIS IS WHERE PORT MODERNISATION MATTERS</h2><p>Nigeria's emerging port strategy could become an important part of that ecosystem.</p><p>The approved modernisation of Onne, Rivers, Delta and Calabar is significant not simply because it creates additional port capacity.</p><p>It creates the possibility of a more distributed maritime economy.</p><p>More cargo corridors.</p><p>More regional logistics activity.</p><p>More opportunities for maritime services.</p><p>More commercial nodes outside Lagos.</p><p>But again, there is a distinction between moving more business through Nigerian ports and owning more of the business that moves through them.</p><p>That distinction should shape how Nigeria measures the success of its maritime reforms.</p><p>Not only:</p><p>How many tonnes moved?</p><p>How many vessels called?</p><p>How much revenue was generated?</p><p>But also:</p><p>How much Nigerian tonnage was created?</p><p>How much Nigerian financing supported it?</p><p>How many Nigerian companies captured freight revenue?</p><p>How much ship repair activity stayed in Nigeria?</p><p>How many high value maritime services were developed locally?</p><p>How much foreign exchange that previously left the country was retained?</p><p>Those are ownership questions.</p><p>And they are economic questions.</p><h2>THE NEW MEASURE OF MARITIME SUCCESS</h2><p>Nigeria does not need to shut foreign companies out of its maritime economy.</p><p>That would be neither practical nor consistent with the country's role in global trade.</p><p>International shipping lines, foreign capital, technology and expertise will remain important.</p><p>The objective is different.</p><p>It is to ensure that Nigerian businesses are capable of competing within that system and capturing a larger share of the value.</p><p>That requires a connected strategy.</p><p>Cabotage creates the policy framework.</p><p>The CVFF provides a financing mechanism.</p><p>Cargo contracts create revenue visibility.</p><p>Banks provide commercial capital.</p><p>Ports provide infrastructure.</p><p>Shipyards and dry docks provide industrial capacity.</p><p>Training institutions provide human capital.</p><p>Regulation provides the rules.</p><p>And enforcement makes those rules credible.</p><p>None of these can deliver maritime economic ownership in isolation.</p><p>The value appears when they work together.</p><h2>SO, WHEN WILL NIGERIA OWN MORE OF THE BUSINESS IT DOES AT SEA?</h2><p>There is no credible date that can simply be announced.</p><p>It will happen when the system begins producing measurable outcomes.</p><p>When Nigerian operators can acquire vessels without spending years searching for viable finance.</p><p>When cargo owners can provide contracts that make those vessels bankable.</p><p>When waivers become a mechanism for genuine capacity gaps rather than a substitute for building capacity.</p><p>When Nigerian shipyards and repair facilities can compete on quality, cost and turnaround.</p><p>When banks treat maritime assets as a specialised commercial sector they understand.</p><p>When port modernisation creates opportunities for Nigerian businesses rather than simply more efficient gateways for foreign operators.</p><p>And when government begins measuring maritime transformation not only by what the sector collects, but by what the Nigerian private sector owns.</p><p>That is the real test.</p><p>Because Nigeria has already demonstrated that it can generate enormous maritime activity.</p><p>It is now time to ask whether it can capture more of the economics behind that activity.</p><h2>PRIMEAXIS INSIGHT</h2><p>Sixty-six years after independence, Nigeria's maritime question is no longer whether the country has the resources to become a major maritime economy.</p><p>It clearly does.</p><p>The question is whether Nigeria can build the financial, industrial and commercial architecture required to own more of the value its trade creates.</p><p>The cargo is here.</p><p>The coastline is here.</p><p>The market is here.</p><p>The policy instruments are here.</p><p>The next requirement is execution.</p><p>And that means moving the conversation from participation to ownership.</p><p>Because maritime independence should not mean that every ship on Nigerian waters must be Nigerian.</p><p>It should mean that a substantially larger share of the business generated by Nigeria's trade can be financed, operated and owned by Nigerians.</p><p>That is the unfinished business.</p><p>And perhaps the most important maritime question of Nigeria's next chapter is not:</p><p>How much cargo can we move?</p><p>It is:</p><p>How much of the value created by moving it will we finally own?</p>
