<h2>Why Inclusion Is Now an Economic Imperative</h2><p class="MsoNormal">Africa's maritime future will not be determined solely by
the ports it builds, the ships it acquires or the policies it adopts. Increasingly,
it will be shaped by something less visible—but far more strategic: the quality
of the human capability behind them.</p>

<p class="MsoNormal">Across the continent, governments are investing in ports,
logistics corridors, inland waterways and Blue Economy initiatives. The African
Continental Free Trade Area (AfCFTA) is accelerating regional trade, while
Africa's Blue Economy—already valued at more than US$300 billion annually—is
expected to play an even greater role in the continent's economic
transformation.</p>

<p class="MsoNormal">Yet amid these investments, one strategic question continues
to receive far less attention than it deserves.</p>

<p class="MsoNormal">Can Africa build world-class maritime capability while
underutilising half of its available talent?</p>

<p class="MsoNormal">For decades, the maritime industry has remained one of the
world's most male-dominated sectors. Women have traditionally been concentrated
in administrative and support roles, while technical operations, seafaring,
marine engineering, ship management and executive leadership remained
overwhelmingly occupied by men. According to the International Maritime
Organization (IMO), women account for only a small fraction of the global
seafaring workforce, while representation across many technical maritime
professions remains disproportionately low.</p>

<p class="MsoNormal">That narrative is gradually changing.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">Across Africa and the wider maritime industry, women are
increasingly serving as ship captains, marine engineers, naval architects, port
managers, maritime lawyers, surveyors, logistics professionals, regulators,
entrepreneurs and technology specialists. Their growing presence reflects more
than progress in gender inclusion. It represents an expansion of the industry's
technical expertise, leadership capacity and innovation potential.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">This distinction matters.</p>

<p class="MsoNormal">Much of the conversation surrounding women in maritime
continues to focus on representation. Representation matters, but
representation alone does not make industries more competitive.</p>

<p class="MsoNormal">Capability does.</p>

<p class="MsoNormal">Every additional marine engineer strengthens engineering
capacity. Every qualified seafarer expands operational capability. Every
logistics specialist improves supply chain performance. Every maritime lawyer
reinforces institutional governance. Every entrepreneur broadens productive
capacity.</p>

<p class="MsoNormal">Viewed through this lens, investing in women is no longer
simply a social objective.</p>

<p class="MsoNormal">It is an economic and strategic imperative.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">The global maritime community increasingly recognises this
reality. The International Maritime Organization (IMO) has consistently
encouraged member states to expand opportunities for women through maritime
education, leadership development and technical cooperation, recognising that a
stronger and more inclusive workforce contributes to safer, more resilient and
more sustainable maritime industries.</p>

<p class="MsoNormal">Across Africa, that vision is being reinforced by a growing
ecosystem of professional organisations committed to developing female maritime
talent. Women in Maritime Africa (WIMA) promotes professional development and
regional collaboration across the continent. Women in Maritime of West and
Central Africa (WIMOWCA) continues to strengthen leadership and cooperation
among maritime professionals within the sub-region. Women's International
Shipping &amp; Trading Association (WISTA), through its national chapters across
Africa, connects executives, entrepreneurs and decision-makers while advancing
leadership within shipping and maritime trade. Likewise, Women in Logistics and
Transport (WiLAT) continues to expand opportunities across logistics, freight
and multimodal transport through mentorship, professional development and
industry advocacy.</p>

<p class="MsoNormal">Collectively, these organisations are doing far more than
advancing gender inclusion. They are strengthening Africa's maritime talent
pipeline, expanding professional networks, nurturing future leaders and
building the human capital required for an industry undergoing profound
transformation.</p>

<p class="MsoNormal">That transformation is being driven by digitalisation,
artificial intelligence, smart ports, decarbonisation, cybersecurity and
increasingly sophisticated global supply chains. Competing in this environment
will require Africa to mobilise the broadest possible pool of technical
expertise.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">The continent's greatest maritime constraint may therefore
not be infrastructure alone.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">It may be human capability.</p>

<p class="MsoNormal">Nigeria offers a useful example of how this thinking is
evolving. For many years, government efforts to advance gender equality in
transport were reflected largely through broad national policy commitments,
with relatively limited sector-specific interventions. More recently, however,
institutions such as the Nigerian Maritime Administration and Safety Agency
(NIMASA) and the Nigerian Ports Authority (NPA) have expanded support for
female cadets through scholarships, technical training, international exposure,
leadership development and professional recognition. While these initiatives
have not eliminated longstanding barriers, they demonstrate a gradual shift
from viewing gender equality as a social aspiration to recognising that
expanding maritime capability requires expanding opportunity.</p>

<p class="MsoNormal">Significant challenges nevertheless remain. Women continue
to be underrepresented in seafaring, marine engineering, offshore operations,
ship ownership and executive leadership. Access to sea-time opportunities,
finance, mentorship, technical training and safe working environments continues
to shape career progression across many parts of the industry.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">These challenges should concern policymakers, regulators,
industry leaders, financial institutions and maritime educators—not simply
because they affect women, but because they affect Africa's competitiveness.</p>

<p class="MsoNormal">Every barrier that prevents capable women from entering or
advancing within maritime reduces the continent's ability to develop the
skilled workforce required for modern shipping, logistics and Blue Economy
industries.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">The question, therefore, is no longer whether women belong
in maritime.</p>

<p class="MsoNormal">Their growing contributions have already answered that.</p>

<p class="MsoNormal">The more important question is whether Africa can build
globally competitive maritime industries while leaving a significant portion of
its available talent underdeveloped.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">History suggests it cannot.</p>

<p class="MsoNormal">The world's leading maritime nations built far more than
ports and fleets. They built institutions that consistently developed technical
expertise, leadership, innovation and specialised knowledge. Infrastructure
created opportunity. Human capability transformed that opportunity into
economic strength.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">Africa's maritime transformation will require the same
approach.</p>

<p class="MsoNormal">Investment in infrastructure must be matched by investment
in people.</p>

<p class="MsoNormal">The continent needs more marine engineers, naval architects,
maritime lawyers, logistics specialists, digital innovators and seafarers.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">Achieving that objective requires expanding opportunity
wherever talent exists.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">That includes women.<o:p></o:p></p><p class="MsoNormal"><br></p>

<h2>PRIMEAXIS INSIGHT<o:p></o:p></h2>

<p class="MsoNormal">Africa's maritime future will not be determined solely by
the depth of its ports, the size of its fleets or the billions invested in the
Blue Economy. Those are important assets, but assets alone do not create
maritime powers.</p>

<p class="MsoNormal">Capability does.</p>

<p class="MsoNormal">For decades, discussions about women in maritime have
centred on representation. While representation remains important, it is no
longer the most strategic conversation.</p>

<p class="MsoNormal">The real conversation is capability.</p>

<p class="MsoNormal">Every female marine engineer developed expands Africa's
engineering capacity.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">Every female seafarer trained strengthens operational
capability.</p>

<p class="MsoNormal">Every female maritime lawyer enhances institutional
governance.</p>

<p class="MsoNormal">Every female logistics professional improves supply chain
resilience.</p>

<p class="MsoNormal">Every female entrepreneur broadens Africa's maritime
enterprise ecosystem.</p>

<p class="MsoNormal"><br></p><p class="MsoNormal">In other words, investing in women is not simply expanding
participation.</p>

<p class="MsoNormal">It is expanding Africa's maritime capability.<o:p></o:p></p>

<p class="MsoNormal">The countries that will lead Africa's maritime future will
not necessarily be those with the longest coastlines or the largest ports. They
will be those that develop the broadest pool of maritime talent, build stronger
institutions and ensure that no source of expertise is left underutilised.</p>

<p class="MsoNormal">Because in the global maritime economy, competitive
advantage is no longer created by geography alone.</p>

<p class="MsoNormal">It is created by capability.</p>

<p class="MsoNormal">No maritime nation ever became globally competitive by
leaving part of its talent behind.<o:p></o:p></p>
