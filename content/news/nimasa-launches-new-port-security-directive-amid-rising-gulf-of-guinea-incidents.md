<p>The Nigerian Maritime Administration and Safety Agency (NIMASA) has issued a new port facility security directive requiring enhanced screening protocols at all federal ports, effective immediately. The directive follows a documented rise in approach-and-boarding attempts recorded across the Gulf of Guinea in the second quarter of 2026.</p><p>Under the directive, terminal operators must implement two-stage identity verification for all shore-side personnel accessing restricted areas, extend closed-circuit surveillance coverage to every berth face, and file monthly facility security assessments with the agency rather than the previous quarterly cycle.</p><p><img src="https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1494412574643-ff11b0a5c1c3?w=1200&amp;q=80&amp;fit=crop" alt="Container terminal operations at a West African port"></p><h3>What Changes for Terminal Operators</h3><p>The most immediate operational impact falls on the container terminals at Apapa and Tin Can Island, where shore-side personnel volumes are highest and access-control queues already constrain gate throughput during peak windows. Operators have been given a 45-day compliance window before enforcement inspections begin.</p><p>NIMASA Director-General confirmed at a briefing in Lagos that the agency will deploy joint inspection teams drawn from the agency, the Nigerian Ports Authority, and the Nigerian Navy. Facilities found non-compliant after the window closes face suspension of their port facility security certificates — a step that would effectively halt international vessel calls at the affected berth.</p><p>The directive also formalises an incident-reporting channel that requires operators to notify the agency within six hours of any unauthorised access attempt, replacing an informal practice that industry participants have long said produced inconsistent data on the true frequency of shore-side security breaches.</p><h3>The Regional Picture</h3><p>The Gulf of Guinea has recorded a measurable increase in reported approach incidents this year, reversing three consecutive years of decline that followed the launch of the Deep Blue Project and expanded regional naval coordination under the Yaoundé Architecture.</p><p>Industry security analysts have attributed part of the increase to a shift in activity from open-water kidnapping toward opportunistic cargo theft at anchorage and within port limits — a pattern that places the burden of response squarely on port facility security rather than on naval patrol assets.</p>
