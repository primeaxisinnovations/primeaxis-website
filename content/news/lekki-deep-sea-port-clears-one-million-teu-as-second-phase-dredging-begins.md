<p>Lekki Deep Sea Port has handled its one-millionth twenty-foot equivalent unit since commercial operations began, the terminal operator confirmed this week, as contractors mobilised for a second phase of channel dredging intended to accommodate larger vessel classes on the Nigerian coast.</p><p>The milestone places the terminal well ahead of the ramp-up curve projected in its original concession documentation, which anticipated cumulative volumes of that scale only in the port's fifth operating year. Terminal management attributed the acceleration to transhipment traffic redirected from congested regional gateways rather than to organic growth in Nigerian import volumes.</p><p><img src="https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1578662996442-48f60103fc96?w=1200&amp;q=80&amp;fit=crop" alt="Gantry cranes working a container vessel at berth"></p><h3>What the Dredging Programme Covers</h3><p>The second phase works will deepen the approach channel and the turning basin, extending the draught envelope available to vessels calling at the terminal. The programme is scheduled across an eighteen-month window, with the contractor working to maintain uninterrupted vessel access throughout.</p><p>Dredging of this kind is rarely a single event. Sediment transport along this stretch of the Bight of Benin means the channel requires a sustained maintenance regime once the capital works conclude — an operating cost that terminal concessions in the region have historically underestimated at the point of financial close.</p><p>Terminal management confirmed that a maintenance dredging contract has been scoped alongside the capital works, structured as a multi-year commitment rather than the campaign-by-campaign approach used at several older Nigerian facilities.</p><h3>The Hinterland Question Remains</h3><p>Berth-side performance has never been the binding constraint on Nigerian container throughput. The connection between the quay and the inland market is.</p><p>The Lekki–Epe corridor carries the overwhelming majority of the terminal's landside container movements, and the completion of the Lekki Port access road has measurably improved truck turn times against the position at commissioning. The rail connection contemplated in the original masterplan, however, remains unbuilt.</p><p><img src="https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1519003722824-194d4455a60c?w=1200&amp;q=80&amp;fit=crop" alt="Freight containers stacked at an inland logistics yard"></p><p>Without it, the terminal's ability to serve the northern Nigerian market — and, more consequentially, the landlocked Sahel states that represent the largest untapped transit corridor available to any Nigerian port — stays bounded by road haulage economics that compare poorly with the rail-served corridors running inland from Tema and Lomé.</p><h3>Regional Competitive Context</h3><p>The West African container market is now materially oversupplied at the berth. Between Lekki, Tema's expanded terminal, Abidjan, Lomé, and Kribi, the coastline has added deep-water capacity faster than regional demand has grown.</p><p>That reality reframes what the one-million-TEU milestone actually signals. In a market where berth capacity is abundant, terminals do not compete on the ability to work a ship. They compete on the total cost and predictability of moving a box from the quay to its final inland destination — customs clearance time, corridor reliability, inland depot availability, and the cost of the last two hundred kilometres.</p><p><img src="https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1590069261209-f8e9b8642343?w=1200&amp;q=80&amp;fit=crop" alt="Aerial view of a deep-water port terminal and access corridor"></p><p>Lekki's operating performance has established that the asset works. The open question for the concession's second decade is whether the corridor infrastructure behind it develops quickly enough to convert that operating performance into durable market share — or whether transhipment volume, which is by nature mobile and price-sensitive, migrates again the moment a neighbouring gateway improves its own economics.</p><h3>What Comes Next</h3><p>The dredging programme runs to a scheduled completion in early 2028. Terminal management indicated that a decision on the second container berth contemplated in the masterplan will follow rather than precede the channel works, tying the expansion commitment to demonstrated demand rather than to projected volumes.</p>
